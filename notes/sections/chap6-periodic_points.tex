\chapter{Periodic Points}
\label{chp:periodic_points}

This chapter is devoted to an extensive discussion of the fixed and periodic
points of a rational map and their role in iteration theory.

% --- SECTION ------------------------------------------------------------------
% --- THE CLASSIFICATION OF PERIODIC POINTS ------------------------------------
\section{The Classification of Periodic Points}
\label{sec:the_classification_of_periodic_points}

We have already used elementary facts about fixed points, and we turn now to a
systematic and detailed discussion of the periodic points of a rational map $R$.
A fixed point $\zeta$ of $R$ is classified according to the \emph{multiplier
$m(R, \zeta)$} of $R$ at $\zeta$ (see \ref{sec:fixed_points}) and as this is
conjugation invariant, we may assume that $\zeta$ is in $\C$ and so $m(R, \zeta)
= R'(\zeta)$. It is important to realize that as the classification of fixed
points is a purely local matter, it applies to any analytic function and, in
particular, to the local inverse (when it exists) of a rational map.

\begin{definition}[Types of fixed points based on the multiplier]
    Suppose that $\zeta$ in $\C$ is a fixed point of an analytic function $f$.
    Then $\zeta$ is:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
        \item \label{itm:supper_attracting_fixed_point} \emph{super-attracting}
        if $f'(\zeta) = 0$;
        \item \label{itm:attracting_fixed_point} \emph{attracting} if $0 <
        |f'(\zeta)| < 1$;
        \item \label{itm:repelling_fixed_point} \emph{repelling} if $|f'(\zeta)|
        > 1$;
        \item \label{itm:rationally_indifferent_fixed_point} \emph{rationally
        indifferent} if $f'(\zeta)$ is a root of unity;
        \item \label{itm:irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point}
        \emph{irrationally indifferent} if $|f'(\zeta)| = 1$, but $|f'(\zeta)|$
        is not a root of unity.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Some explanatory remarks may be helpful. First, the distinction between
\ref{itm:supper_attracting_fixed_point} and \ref{itm:attracting_fixed_point} is
that a super-attracting fixed point is a critical point of $f$, whereas an
attracting fixed point is not; thus $f$ has a local inverse near $\zeta$ if
$\zeta$ is attracting but not if it is super-attracting. The reader may recall
that in section \ref{sec:introduction} no such distinction was made and, of
course, there are still occasions when we wish to embrace both cases; we do this
by saying that $\zeta$ is \emph{(super)attracting}. Similarly, it is convenient
to combine cases \ref{itm:rationally_indifferent_fixed_point} and
\ref{itm:irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point}, and such a fixed point is said
to be \emph{indifferent}. For an indifferent fixed point $\zeta$, the best
linear approximation of $f$ near $\zeta$ is a rotation about $\zeta$ and the
distinction between \ref{itm:rationally_indifferent_fixed_point} and
\ref{itm:irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point} is simply whether this rotation
has finite or infinite order.

The notion of periodic points, and the extension of the classification to these,
is straightforward. As we already defined in
Definition~\ref{def:periodic_point}, a point $\zeta$ is a \emph{periodic point}
of a rational function $R$ if it is a fixed point of some iterate $R^m$. For
such a point $\zeta$ there is a positive integer $n$ for which
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cycle_of_a_periodic_point}
    \zeta, R(\zeta), R^2(\zeta), \dots, R^{n-1}(\zeta)
\end{equation}
are distinct but $R^n(\zeta) = \zeta$.

\begin{definition}[Cycle of a periodic point]
    The finite set of points in \eqref{eq:cycle_of_a_periodic_point} is the
    \emph{cycle of $\zeta$}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Period of a periodic point]
    The integer $n$ is the \emph{period} of $\zeta$.
\end{definition}

Of course, the fixed points of $R$ are points of period one: more generally,
$\zeta$ has period $n$ if and only if it is a fixed point of $R^n$ but not of
any lower-order iterate.

\begin{remark}[$(R^n)'(\zeta_m) = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} R'(\zeta_k)$]
    A periodic point $\zeta$ of period $n$ is classified as a fixed point of
    $R^n$, but we can say a little more than this. By conjugation, we may assume
    that the cycle does not contain $\infty$, and we write
    \begin{equation*}
        \zeta_m = R^m(\zeta), \quad m = 0, 1, 2, \dots,
    \end{equation*}
    so $\zeta_{m+n} = \zeta_m$. By $n$ applications of the Chain Rule, we now
    have
    \begin{align*}
        (R^n)'(\zeta_m) &= \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} R'(R^k(\zeta_m))
                        &= \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} R'(\zeta_k),
    \end{align*}
    the product being a re-arrangement of the first. This argument shows that
    the derivative $(R^n)'$ has the same value at each point $\zeta_j$ of the
    cycle.
\end{remark}

As a consequence of this, we can extend our classification to cycles and speak
naturally of the \emph{multiplier of the cycle}, \emph{attracting cycles} and so
on.

\begin{definition}[Multiplier of $R$ on a cycle]
    We define the multiplier of a cycle as the multiplier of $R$ on any point of
    the cycle (all of them have the same multiplier).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Types of cycles based on the multiplier]
    We say a cycle is \emph{super-attracting}, \emph{attracting},
    \emph{repelling}, \emph{rationally indifferent} or \emph{irrationally
    indifferent} depending on the type of its points (all of them are of the
    same type).
\end{definition}

As we already defined in Definition~\ref{def:periodic_point}, a point $\zeta$ is
\emph{pre-periodic} under $R$ if it is not periodic but if some image, say
$R^m(\zeta)$, is: in this case there exist positive integers $m$ and $n$ with
\begin{equation*}
    \zeta, R(\zeta), R^2(\zeta), \dots, R^m(\zeta), \dots, R^{m+n-1}(\zeta)
\end{equation*}
distinct but with $R^{m+n}(\zeta) = R^m(\zeta)$. Most periodic points have
associated pre-periodic points.

\begin{example}
    For example, the origin is a pre-periodic point of the polynomial $z^2 - 2$.
\end{example}

In the remainder of this chapter, we shall discuss how the attracting, repelling
and indifferent cycles relate to the Fatou and Julia sets.

\begin{remark}[(Super)Attracting points are in $F$ and repelling points are in $J$]
    Briefly, the (super)attracting cycles are in the Fatou set, while the
    repelling cycles are in the Julia set.
\end{remark}

The corresponding results for indifferent cycles are more delicate and require
much more work.

\section{The Existence of Periodic Points}
\label{sec:the_existence_of_periodic_points}

In an attempt to find the periodic points of period $n$ of a rational map $R$,
we naturally consider the solutions of
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:periodic_points_equation}
    R^n(z) = z.
\end{equation}
Any solution of this equation will be periodic; however, it will not necessarily
be of period $n$ for it may already be fixed by some earlier iterate $R^m$,
where $m < n$.

\begin{example}
    This occurs, for example, when
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:z_squared_minus_z}
        R(z) = z^2 - z:
    \end{equation}
    in this case every solution of $R^2(z) = z$ is also a solution of $R(z) = z$
    and so for this $R$, \emph{there are no periodic points of period $2$}. The
    Julia set of $z^2 - z$ is shown in
    Figure~\ref{fig:z_squared_minus_z_julia_set}.
\end{example}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{z_squared_minus_z_julia_set-thin.pdf}
    \caption{Julia set of $z \mapsto z^2 - z$.}
    \label{fig:z_squared_minus_z_julia_set}
\end{figure}

This example raises a question of existence, namely: How often are periodic
points absent? Could there exist, for example, a rational function with only
finitely many periodic points? The answer is no. In fact, in the vast majority
of cases, periodic points of a given period do exist and for polynomials, the
situation is particularly striking. We have

\begin{theorem}[Existence of periodic points for polynomials]
    \label{thm:existence_of_periodic_points_for_polynomials_maps}
    Let $P$ be a polynomial of degree at least two and suppose that $P$ has no
    periodic points of period $n$. Then $n = 2$ and $P$ is conjugate to $z
    \mapsto z^2 - z$.
\end{theorem}

In short, the example given in \eqref{eq:z_squared_minus_z} was the only example
of this type (up to conjugacy) that we could possibly have given. There is also
a corresponding (though slightly weaker) result for rational functions, namely

\begin{theorem}[Existence of periodic points for rational maps]
    \label{thm:existence_of_periodic_points_for_rational_maps}
    Let $R$ be a rational function of degree $d$, where $d \geq 2$, and suppose
    that $R$ has no periodic points of period $n$. Then $(d, n)$ is one of the
    pairs
    \begin{equation*}
        (2, 2), (2, 3), (3, 2), (4, 2);
    \end{equation*}
    moreover, each such pair does arise from some $R$ in this way.
\end{theorem}

This shows for example, that all rational functions have periodic points of
period $4, 5, 6, \dots$, and that every rational function of degree five or more
has periodic points of all orders. These results are due to I.N. Baker
\cite{Baker1964}, and we shall prove them in
section~\ref{sec:the_proof_of_the_existence_of_periodic_points}. Roughly
speaking, if a rational map fails to have any periodic points of period $n$,
then every solution of $R^n(z) = z$ must also be a solution of some equation
$R^m(z) = z$ for some $m$ which divides, and is less than, $n$. This places a
strong algebraic constraint on the coefficients of $R$ which, according to the
two results above, is rarely satisfied; nevertheless, the known proofs of these
results are function-theoretic.

The reader should note that not only is
Theorem~\ref{thm:existence_of_periodic_points_for_rational_maps} weaker than
Theorem~\ref{thm:existence_of_periodic_points_for_polynomials_maps} in that
several exceptional pairs $(d, n)$ arise, but is also weaker in that an
exceptional pair $(d, n)$ does not necessarily determine a unique conjugacy
class of exceptional maps $R$.

\begin{remark}[Each rational map has periodic points with prime period $p$ for all but at most $d + 1$ prime numbers $p$]
    Although we shall prove
    Theorem~\ref{thm:existence_of_periodic_points_for_polynomials_maps} and
    Theorem~\ref{thm:existence_of_periodic_points_for_rational_maps} later, we
    shall give a simple argument now which guarantees the existence of at least
    an infinite set of periodic points for each rational map (a fact that we
    shall need before we prove the two theorems). This also follows from
    Theorem~\ref{thm:J_contained_in_closure_of_periodic_points_set}, but the
    following argument is simple and direct. We suppose that $R$ is a rational
    function of degree $d$, where $d \geq 2$, and that $R$ has no points of
    period $p$, where $p$ is a prime. Then every solution $\zeta$, say, of
    $R^p(z) = z$ is of some period $k$, where $k$ is less than, and divides $p$:
    thus $k$ = 1, and $\zeta$ is a fixed point of $R$ itself. As $R$ and $R^p$
    have divided $d + 1$ and $d^p + 1$ fixed points respectively, we deduce that
    there must be some fixed point $\zeta$ of $R$ such that $R^p$ has more fixed
    points at $\zeta$ than $R$ does, and the only way that this can arise is
    that $R'(\zeta) \neq 1$ but $R'(\zeta)^p = 1$ (see
    Corollary~\ref{cor:fn_has_at_least_as_many_fixed_points_as_f}). Finally,
    there are at most $d + 1$ fixed points $\zeta$ fixed by $R$ and for each of
    these, $R'(\zeta)^q = 1$ for at most one prime $q$. Thus, \emph{each
    rational map has periodic points with prime period $p$ for all but at most
    $d + 1$ prime numbers $p$}.
\end{remark}

If we consider this argument when $R$ is a quadratic polynomial with no periodic
points of period two, we find that up to conjugacy $R(0) = 0$ and $R'(0) = -1$.
It follows that $R(z) = az^2 - z$, and just by changing the scale, this is
easily seen to be conjugate to the polynomial $z^2 - z$ in
\eqref{eq:z_squared_minus_z}.

\section{(Super)Attracting Cycles}
\label{sec:attracting_cycles}

Suppose that $\zeta$ is a (super)attracting fixed point of a rational map $R$.
Then $|R'(\zeta)| < 1$ and there is a number $\alpha$, $|R'(\zeta)| < \alpha <
1$, and a disc $D$ centered at $\zeta$ such that in $D$,
\begin{equation*}
    |R(z) - \zeta| = |R(z) - R(\zeta)| < \alpha |z - \zeta|.
\end{equation*}
This shows that each $R^n$ maps $D$ into itself, so $D$ lies in the Fatou set
$F(R)$ and $|R^n| \to \zeta$ uniformly on $D$. By Vitali's Theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:Vitalis_theorem}), $R^n \to \zeta$ locally uniformly on the
component $F(R)$ which contains $\zeta$, and as $F(R^q) = F(R)$ for any iterate
$R^q$, we easily obtain

\begin{theorem}[Local basins of a (super)attracting cycle]
    \label{thm:local_basins_of_a_superattracting_cycle}
    Let $\{\zeta_1, \dots, \zeta_q\}$ be a (super)attracting cycle of $R$. Then
    each $\zeta_j$ lies in a component, say $F_j$, of the Fatou set $F(R)$, and as
    $n \to \infty$, $(R^q)^n \to \zeta_j$ locally uniformly on $F_j$.
\end{theorem}

Thus, each point of the cycle must lie in a distinct Fatou component, as every
point in $F_j$ converges to $\zeta_j$. In view of this, we introduce some
terminology.

\begin{definition}[Local or immediate basin of a (super)attracting fixed point]
    Given a (super)attracting fixed point $\zeta$ of $R$, the component of
    $F(R)$ which contains $\zeta$ is called the \emph{local}, or
    \emph{immediate}, \emph{basin} of $\zeta$.
\end{definition}

It follows that $R^n \to \zeta$ precisely when $z$ lies in some inverse image
$R^{-m}(B)$, $m \geq 0$, of the local basin $B$.

\begin{definition}[Basin or stable set of a (super)attracting fixed point]
    We call the set of such $z$ the \emph{basin} (or \emph{stable set}) for
    $\zeta$. 
\end{definition}

More generally, for cycles of any period,

\begin{definition}[Local basin of a (super)attracting cycle]
    The \emph{local basin of a (super)attracting cycle $\{\zeta_1, \dots,
    \zeta_q\}$} is the union of the (necessarily distinct) components $F_1,
    \dots, F_q$ of $F(R)$ (as in
    Theorem~\ref{thm:local_basins_of_a_superattracting_cycle})
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Basin of a (super)attracting cycle]
    And the \emph{basin} of the cycle is the union of the local basins and all
    its inverse images.
\end{definition}

Although a rational map $R$ of degree $d$, $d \geq 2$, has infinitely many
cycles, we shall see later that it can have \emph{only finitely many
(super)attracting} cycles (in fact, at most $2d - 2$). Thus, for example, a
quadratic polynomial can have at most one (super)attracting cycle in $\C$ (the
reader may recall that in section~\ref{sec:iteration_of_z_squared_plus_c} we
showed that a quadratic polynomial can have at most one attracting fixed point
in $\C$).

Our next task is to analyze the behavior of an analytic function $f$ in a
neighborhood of an attracting fixed point $\zeta$, and this analysis will enable
us to understand completely the dynamics of $f$ near $\zeta$. We suppose, then,
that $f$ is analytic in some neighborhood $N$ of an attracting fixed point
$\zeta$, and we may assume that $f(N) \subset N$. Now let $g$ be any function
which is analytic and univalent on $N$ with $g(\zeta) = 0$, and let $F = g f
g^{-1}$. Then $F$ is defined and analytic on the neighborhood $g(N)$ of the
origin (see Figure~\ref{fig:figure_6_3_1}) and, as $F'(0) = f'(\zeta)$, we see
that $0$ is an attracting fixed point of $F$.

\begin{definition}[Local analytic conjugate]
    We call $F$ a \emph{local (analytic) conjugate} of $f$.
\end{definition}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{beardons_book/figure_6_3_1.png}
    \caption{}
    \label{fig:figure_6_3_1}
\end{figure}

And we ask whether, by choosing $g$ suitably, we can arrange for $F$ to be
considerably simpler (say, in terms of Taylor coefficients) than $f$ is. The
benefits that arise depend on how simple we can choose $F$ to be but, in any
case, the iterates of $f$ (near $\zeta$) are reflected in the behavior of the
iterates of $F$ for $F^n = g f^n g^{-1}$.

We shall assume that $\zeta = 0$: this simplifies the formulae, and the
corresponding results for a general fixed point $\zeta$ can easily be obtained
afterward by conjugation. The function $f$ has a Taylor expansion at $0$, say
\begin{equation*}
    f(z) = az + b z^q + \dots , \quad 0 < |a| < 1,
\end{equation*}
and because $F'(0) = f'(\zeta)$, we must have
\begin{equation*}
    F(z) = az + b_2 z^2 + b_3 z^3 + \dots .
\end{equation*}
We shall now show that by choosing a suitable $g$, we can achieve the greatest
possible simplification, namely that each $b_j$ is zero and $F$ is the map $z
\mapsto az$.

\begin{theorem}[Koenigs' local linearization theorem]
    \label{thm:koenigs_local_linearization}
    Suppose that $f$ is analytic in a neighborhood of the origin, that $f(0) =
    0$, and that $f'(0) = a$ where $0 < |a| < 1$. Then there exists a unique
    function $g$ which is analytic in some disc $\{|z| < r\}$ with $g(0) = 0$,
    $g'(0) = 1$, and which satisfies $g f g^{-1} = az$ for all $z$ sufficiently
    near the origin.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[106]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

In fact, this result implies more than we have stated so far.

\begin{remark}[$B = W \cup W_1 \cup W_2 \cup \cdots$]
    We consider the case when $f$ is a rational function, which we now denote by
    $R$, and we let $B$ be the local basin of an attracting fixed point $\zeta$
    of $R$. According to Theorem~\ref{thm:koenigs_local_linearization}, there is
    a disc $W$ centered at $\zeta$ such that $R(W) \subset W$, and a power
    series $g$ analytic in $W$ such that $g R(z) = a g(z)$ there. Now let $W_n$
    be the component of $R^{-n}(W)$ that contains $\zeta$: we claim that
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:local_basin_formula}
        B = W \cup W_1 \cup W_2 \cup \cdots .
    \end{equation}
\end{remark}
\begin{proof}
    First, as $W_n$ is a connected subset of $F(R)$, it must lie within $B$.
    Next, given any $z$ in $B$, join $z$ to $\zeta$ by a curve $\gamma$ in $B$.
    Then by Theorem~\ref{thm:local_basins_of_a_superattracting_cycle}, there is
    some $n$ such that $R^n(\gamma) \subset W$, and hence $\gamma$, being
    connected, lies in $W_n$. This shows that $z$ is in $W_n$ and thus proves
    \eqref{eq:local_basin_formula}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Analytic continuation of $g$ to local basin]
    Now we define $g_n$ on $W_n$ by the formula
    \begin{equation*}
        g_n(z) = a^{-n} g(R^n z).
    \end{equation*}
    Clearly, $g_n$ is analytic on $W_n$ and for all $z$ sufficiently close to
    the origin, $g_n(z) = g(z)$. We deduce that the sequence $g_n$ provides an
    analytic continuation of $g$ to the entire local basin $B$, and by analytic
    continuation, $g R(z) = a g(z)$ throughout $B$.
\end{remark}

Finally, we observe that
\begin{equation*}
    g(W_n) = a^{-n} g(R^n(W_n)),
\end{equation*}
and as $R^n$ maps each component of $R^{-n}(W)$ onto $W$, we have
\begin{equation*}
    g(W_n) = a^{-n} g(W).
\end{equation*}
Now $g(W)$ is an open neighborhood of the origin, and as $|a| < 1$, we easily
deduce that $g(B) = \C$, and we have proved

\begin{theorem}[Koenigs' global linearization theorem]
    \label{thm:koenigs_global_linearization}
    Let $\zeta$ be an attracting fixed point of the rational map $R$ with
    $R'(\zeta) = a$, and let $B$ be the local basin of $\zeta$. Then there
    exists a unique function $g$ that is analytic in $B$ with $g(\zeta) = 0$,
    $g'(\zeta) = 1$, and $g(B) = \C$, and such that in $B$, $g R(z) = a g(z)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[108--109]{Beardon1991}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[$g^{-1}$ does not exist throughout $B$]
    Unlike the local conjugacy relation $g R g^{-1}(z) = az$, the functional
    equation $g R(z) = a g(z)$ does not require the existence of $g^{-1}$
    (indeed, by Liouville's Theorem, $g^{-1}$ cannot exist throughout $B$). The
    analysis of these equations and their solutions is attributed to Poincaré,
    Schröder and Koenigs.
\end{remark}

\section{Repelling Cycles}
\label{sec:repelling_cycles}

We begin with

\begin{theorem}[Repelling cycles are in $J$]
    \label{thm:repelling_cycles_are_in_J}
    For any rational map $R$, every repelling cycle of $R$ lies in $J(R)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[109--110]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

We can also interpret Theorem~\ref{thm:koenigs_global_linearization} in the
context of repelling fixed points. Suppose that $\zeta$ is a repelling fixed
point of the rational map $R$, which implies that $R(\zeta) = \zeta$ and
$|R'(\zeta)| > 1$. Because the derivative $R'(\zeta)$ is non-zero, the Inverse
Function Theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:inverse_function_theorem_in_C}) guarantees
that a local branch of the inverse map, $R^{-1}$, exists in a small neighborhood
of $\zeta$. This inverse branch also fixes $\zeta$ (since $R^{-1}(\zeta) =
\zeta$), and its derivative at $\zeta$ is given by the reciprocal of the
original derivative:
\begin{equation*}
    (R^{-1})'(\zeta) = \frac{1}{R'(\zeta)}.
\end{equation*}
Because $|R'(\zeta)| > 1$, it follows that $|(R^{-1})'(\zeta)| < 1$.
Consequently, $\zeta$ acts as an \emph{attracting} fixed point for this local
inverse branch $R^{-1}$. 

We can now directly apply Theorem~\ref{thm:koenigs_local_linearization} to this
attracting branch $R^{-1}$. This provides a local analytic change of coordinates
$g$ such that $R^{-1}$ is conjugate to a strict linear contraction near the
origin:
\begin{equation*}
    g R^{-1} g^{-1}(z) = \frac{1}{R'(\zeta)} z.
\end{equation*}
By taking the algebraic inverse of both sides of this coordinate equation, we
can undo the effect of $R^{-1}$ and return to our original map. This
demonstrates that $R$ itself is locally conjugate to a linear expansion, namely
\begin{equation*}
    g R g^{-1}(z) = R'(\zeta) z,
\end{equation*}
valid in a sufficiently small neighborhood of the repelling fixed point $\zeta$.

\section{Rationally Indifferent Cycles}
\label{sec:rationally_indifferent_cycles}

Although it is not easy to decide whether a given indifferent cycle lies in the
Fatou or the Julia set, the question is easily settled for rationally
indifferent cycles.

\begin{theorem}[Rationally indifferent cycles are in $J$]
    If $\deg(R) \geq 2$ then every rationally indifferent cycle of $R$ lies in
    $J(R)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[111]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

The remainder of this section is devoted to a detailed description of the
dynamics of the iterates $R^n$ near rationally indifferent cycles. This is a
long and difficult task, and in order to obtain a clear understanding of the
global structure of the arguments, we have provided a succession of results,
each (generally speaking) being an improvement on the preceding results. One of
the reasons why this description is much harder to obtain than, say, the
description of the dynamics near an attracting fixed point, is that a rational
map $R$ is \emph{not} locally conjugate to its (linear) derivative near a
rationally indifferent point $\zeta$. Because the derivative is a rotation of
finite order about $\zeta$ ($|R'(\zeta)| = 1 \implies R'(\zeta) = \exp(i
\theta)$ because $\zeta$ is indifferent, and it is a root of the unity), and $R$
can have finite order only if $\deg(R) = 1$ (because if $R$ has finite order,
there exists a natural number $k$ such that $R^k = \Id$, thus, $\deg(R^k) =
\deg(R)^k = \deg(\Id) = 1$). This observation means that we have to find another
way to describe the dynamics of the $R^n$ near such a cycle, and the answer lies
in showing that $R$ is conjugate to a translation not, of course, on the whole
neighborhood of $\zeta$, but on certain open sets which have $\zeta$ on their
boundary. We shall include examples to illustrate the ideas as we proceed, and
we begin with a simple lemma which isolates the most basic idea in this section.

\begin{lemma}[Alternating attracting and repelling sectors for a rationally indifferent point]
    \label{lem:alternating_attracting_and_repelling_sectors_for_a_rationally_indifferent_point}
    Suppose that $f$ is analytic and satisfies
    \begin{equation*}
        f(z) = z - z^{p+1} + O(z^{p+2})
    \end{equation*}
    in some neighborhood $N$ of the origin. Let $\omega_1, \dots, \omega_p$ be
    the $p$-th roots of unity and let $\eta_1, \dots, \eta_n$ be the $p$-th
    roots of $-1$. Then for sufficiently small positive numbers $r_0$ and
    $\theta_0$:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item $|f(z)| < |z|$ on each sector
        \begin{equation*}
            S_j = \left\{z \colon 0 < \left|\frac{z}{\omega_j}\right| < r_0, \left|\arg\left(\frac{z}{\omega_j}\right)\right| < \theta_0\right\};
        \end{equation*}
        and
        \item $|f(z)| > |z|$ on each sector
        \begin{equation*}
            \Sigma_j = \left\{z \colon 0 < \left|\frac{z}{\eta_j}\right| < r_0, \left|\arg\left(\frac{z}{\eta_j}\right)\right| < \theta_0\right\}.
        \end{equation*}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[112]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

The numbers $\omega_j$ and $\eta_j$ in
Lemma~\ref{lem:alternating_attracting_and_repelling_sectors_for_a_rationally_indifferent_point}
are the $2p$-th roots of unity, and they are alternately and equally spaced
around the unit circle (see
Figure~\ref{fig:alternating_attracting_and_repelling_sectors_for_a_rationally_indifferent_point}).
If
\begin{equation*}
    f(z) = z + az^{p+1} + O(z^{p+2}), \quad a \neq 0,
\end{equation*}
a similar result holds with the directions $\omega_j$ and $\eta_j$ replaced by
the directions of the solutions $az^p = -1$ and $az^p = 1$ respectively. Also
with this $f$, $f^{-1}$ exists near the origin and
\begin{equation*}
    f^{-1}(z) = z - az^{p+1} + O(z^{p+2})
\end{equation*}
so (with $r_0$ and $\theta_0$ small enough) $|f^{-1}(z)| > |z|$ on each $S_j$
and $|f^{-1}(z)| < |z|$ on each $\Sigma_j$.

Next, we consider a simple example and here our discussion contains the ideas
that we shall use to develop the general theory.

\begin{example}
    The polynomial
    \begin{equation*}
        P(z) = z - z^2
    \end{equation*}
    has a rationally indifferent fixed point at the origin. If $0 < x < 1$, then
    $0 < P(x) < x$, and so $P^n(x) \to 0$ (for $P^n(x)$ can only converge to a
    fixed point of $P$): if $x < 0$, then $P(x) < x < 0$, and so $P^n(x) \to
    \infty$. These observations are consistent with
    Lemma~\ref{lem:alternating_attracting_and_repelling_sectors_for_a_rationally_indifferent_point},
    and we shall now investigate the behavior of $P^n(z)$ for all $z$ near the
    origin.

    Let $\sigma(z) = 1/z$ and replace $P$ by its conjugate $Q = \sigma P
    \sigma^{-1}$: thus
    \begin{equation*}
        Q(z) = z + 1 + \frac{1}{z-1}.
    \end{equation*}
    Now note that if $\real{z} > 1$ then $\real{(z-1)^{-1}} > 0$ so
    \begin{equation*}
        \real{Q(z)} \geq \real{z} + 1.
    \end{equation*}
    In particular, if $t > 1$, then $Q$ maps $\{x + iy \mid x > t\}$ into $\{x +
    iy \mid x > t + 1\}$, and rewriting this in terms of $P$, we see that $P^n
    \to 0$ uniformly in each sufficiently small disc $\{z \colon |z - r| < r\}$.
    In fact, $P$ acts as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:figure_6_5_1} (which the
    reader should compare with
    Figure~\ref{fig:action_of_z_plus_one_over_z_near_infinity}).
\end{example}

In order to analyze the situation for a general rationally indifferent fixed
point, we need the notion of petals (which, roughly speaking, play the role of
the region $\sigma(\Pi)$ in the example above).

\begin{definition}[Petal]
    For each positive $t$, each positive integer $p$ and each $k$ in $\{0, 1,
    \dots, p-1\}$, we define the sets
    \begin{equation*}
        \Pi_k(t) = \left\{r e^{i\theta} \middle| r^p < t(1 + \cos(p\theta)); \left|\frac{2k\pi}{p - \theta}\right| < \frac{\pi}{p}\right\}.
    \end{equation*}
    These sets are called \emph{petals} (at the origin).
\end{definition}

We can see an illustration of these petals when $p = 6$ in
Figure~\ref{fig:figure_6_5_4}. Note that the petals are pairwise disjoint, and
that each subtends an angle $2\pi/p$ at the origin (so the total angle subtended
at the origin by all the petals is $2\pi$).

\begin{definition}[Axis of a petal]
    We call the line of symmetry of $\Pi_k(t)$ (the ray $\theta = 2k\pi/p$) the
    \emph{axis} of $\Pi_k$.
\end{definition}

We come now to the first major result of this section and in this, we describe
the dynamics of the iterates $R^n$ in the petals at a fixed point $\zeta$ where
$R'(\zeta) = 1$. By conjugation, we may assume that $\zeta = 0$ and, in
addition, we shall assume that $R$ has a Taylor expansion there of the form
\begin{equation*}
    R(z) = z(1 - z^p + bz^{2p} + cz^{2p+1} + \cdots).
\end{equation*}
Of course, we can always ensure that $z^{p+1}$ has coefficient $-1$ simply by
conjugating $R$ with a map of the form $z \mapsto \alpha z$: thus \emph{the
significant part of this assumption is that there are no terms between $z^{p+1}$
and $z^{2p+1}$ in the Taylor series for $R$}. We emphasize that the role of this
assumption is that it enables us to give an \emph{explicit description of the
petals}; without it, we can only establish the existence of petal shaped regions
which are conformal images of $\Pi_k(t)$ described above.

Despite the fact that our interest centers on rational functions, it is
desirable to prove the result for general analytic functions, for we can then
apply it to branches $R^{-1}$. We now prove

\begin{theorem}[The Petal Theorem]
    \label{thm:the_petal_theorem}
    Suppose that the analytic map $f$ has a Taylor expansion
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:taylor_expansion_for_the_petal_theorem}
        f(z) = z - z^{p+1} + O(z^{2p+1})
    \end{equation}
    at the origin. Then for all sufficiently small $t$:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item \label{itm:petal_forward_invariance} $f$ maps each petal
        $\Pi_k(t)$ into itself;
        \item \label{itm:iterates_converge_to_zero_uniformly} $f^n(z) \to 0$
        uniformly on each petal as $n \to \infty$;
        \item \label{itm:iterates_convergence_to_zero_along_petal_axis}
        $\arg(f^n(z)) \to 2k\pi/p$ locally uniformly on $\Pi_k$ as $n \to
        \infty$;
        \item \label{itm:attracting_behavior_on_the_petals_axis} $|f(z)| < |z|$
        on a neighborhood of the axis of each petal;
        \item \label{itm:linear_behavior_in_the_petals} $f: \Pi_k(t) \to
        \Pi_k(t)$ is conjugate to a translation.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[116--122]{Beardon1991}.

    In the proof the following lemmas are used.

    Lemma[Forward Invariance and Translation of $\Pi$]:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item $\Pi$ is forward invariant under $g$;
        \item $\real{g^n(w)} \to +\infty$ uniformly on $\Pi$;
        \item $g: \Pi \to \Pi$ is conjugate to a translation.
    \end{enumerate}

    Proof: \cite[118--122]{Beardon1991}

    Lemma[Local Uniform Convergence of $u_n$]: $u_n$ converges locally uniformly
    on $\Pi$ to some function $u$ that is holomorphic and univalent on $\Pi$.
    
    Proof: \cite[122]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

By \ref{itm:petal_forward_invariance} the iterates $f^n$ are defined on each
$\Pi_k(t)$, and \ref{itm:iterates_convergence_to_zero_along_petal_axis} implies
that for any $z$ in $\Pi_k(t)$, the sequence $f^n(z)$ converges to $0$ along a
path which is asymptotic to the axis of the petal $\Pi_k(t)$: see
Figure~\ref{fig:figure_6_5_5}.

We recall that Theorem~\ref{thm:the_petal_theorem} has only been proved under
the assumption~\eqref{eq:taylor_expansion_for_the_petal_theorem} on the
coefficients of $R$. The next step is to prove the corresponding result for
functions $f$ which satisfy $f(0) = 0$ and $f'(0) = 1$, but not the
condition~\eqref{eq:taylor_expansion_for_the_petal_theorem}, and for this we
need the following result.

\begin{theorem}[Condition relaxation for the Petal Theorem]
    \label{thm:condition_relaxation_for_the_petal_theorem}
    Suppose that $f$ is analytic near $0$ with
    \begin{equation*}
        f(z) = z + az^{p+1} + O(z^{p+2}), \quad a \neq 0,
    \end{equation*}
    as $z \to 0$. Then $f$ is conjugate near $0$ to a function
    \begin{equation*}
        F(z) = z - z^{p+1} + O(z^{2p+1}).
    \end{equation*}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[123--124]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[First generalization of the Petal Theorem, still $R'(0) = 1$]
    With this available, the existence of petals (and the dynamics of $f^n$ on
    these petals) for analytic maps of the form
    \begin{equation*}
        f(z) = z + a z^{p+1} + \cdots,
    \end{equation*}
    follows immediately. Indeed, by
    Theorem~\ref{thm:condition_relaxation_for_the_petal_theorem}, such and $f$
    is conjugate to some $F$ near the origin, and
    Theorem~\ref{thm:the_petal_theorem} is applicable to $F$; thus we can define
    the petals for $f$ as the conformal images (under the conjugation map) of
    the petals of $F$. We shall not give an explicit statement of this result,
    and it suffices to remark that the conclusions of
    Theorem~\ref{thm:the_petal_theorem} are valid (using
    Lemma~\ref{lem:alternating_attracting_and_repelling_sectors_for_a_rationally_indifferent_point}
    to justify \ref{itm:attracting_behavior_on_the_petals_axis}) in these more
    general circumstances except for the fact that we no longer have an explicit
    expression for the petals. We do, however, know that each petal for $f$
    subtends an angle $2\pi/p$ at the origin, and that the petals are pairwise
    disjoint (if taken small enough) for the function conjugating $f$ to $F$ is
    conformal there.
\end{remark}

Theorems~\ref{thm:the_petal_theorem} and
\ref{thm:condition_relaxation_for_the_petal_theorem} describe the action of the
iterates of any map of the form
\begin{equation*}
    f(z) = z + az^{p+1} + \cdots
\end{equation*}
on the $p$ petals of $f$ at the origin. We now consider the case when $f$ is a
rational map $R$ and obtain information about the relationship of the petals to
the Fatou set of $R$.

\begin{theorem}[Relation between petals and the Fatou set]
    \label{thm:relation_between_petals_and_fatou_set}
    Let $R$ be a rational map and suppose that
    \begin{equation*}
        R(z) = z + az^{p+1} + \cdots, \quad a \neq 0,
    \end{equation*}
    near the origin. Let $\Pi_j$ be the petals of $R$ and, for each $j$, let
    $F_j$ be the component of $F(R)$ that contains $\Pi_j$. Then:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item $R^n(z) \to 0$ and $\arg(R^n(z)) \to 2\pi k/p$ on $F_k$; and
        \item $F_0, \dots, F_{p-1}$ are distinct components of $F(R)$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[126]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[126--128]{Beardon1991}.

    In the proof the following lemmas are used.

    Lemma[Normality of Iterates in W]: $\{\varphi_n\}$ is normal in $W$.

    Proof: See \cite[127]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[128--130]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

The geometric picture, then, is as follows. The rationally indifferent fixed
point $0$ lies on the boundary of $p$ components $F_k$ of $F(R)$, and each $F_k$
contains a petal $\Pi_k$ which makes an angle $2\pi/p$ at $0$. Next, $R$ maps
each $F_k$ into itself, and $R^n(z) \to 0$ locally uniformly on each $F_k$ in
such a way that for any $z$ in $F_k$, $R^n(z) \to 0$ on a path which is
asymptotic to the axis of the petal $\Pi_k$. It is an immediate consequence of
this that different petals lie in different components of $F(R)$. In addition,
this convergence shows that for any $z$, $R^n(z)$ ultimately lies within
$\Pi_k$, so the consecutive inverse images of the petal $\Pi_k$ in $F_k$ expand
to fill the entire component $F_k$.

Finally, we note that a rationally indifferent fixed point may also lie on the
boundary of components of $F$ other than the $F_j$: indeed, if $P(z) = z +
\cdots$ is any non-linear polynomial, then the origin is not only on the
boundary of each of the $p$ components $F_j$, but it is also on the boundary of
the completely invariant component $F_\infty$ of $F$ which contains $\infty$
(and which is distinct from each $F_j$ because $P^n \to \infty$ on $F_\infty$).
As $F_\infty$ is connected, it must therefore contain a thin \enquote{tongue}
which stretches to the origin \emph{between} the petals: see
Figure~\ref{fig:figure_6_5_8} (where $p = 5$) and Figure~\ref{fig:figure_6_5_9}
(where $p = 3$) in which the petals are colored black.

\begin{remark}[Second generalization of the Petal Theorem]
    There is still one more result that we must consider before we can give the
    Petal Theorem in its full generality, namely the extension of
    \ref{thm:relation_between_petals_and_fatou_set} to the case when $R(0) = 0$
    and $R'(0)$ is a root of unity other than $1$. We assume, then, that
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:taylor_expansion_for_the_general_petal_theorem}
        R(z) = az + bz^{p+1} + \cdots,
    \end{equation}
    where $a \neq 1$ but $a = \exp(2\pi i r/q)$ where $r$ and $q$ are coprime
    (so $a^n = 1$ when $n = q$ but not for any smaller $n$). In this case, $R^q$
    is of the form
    \begin{equation*}
        R^q(z) = z + cz^{t+1} + \cdots
    \end{equation*}
    for some $t$, so $R^q$ has $t$ petals at the origin. This means that there
    are components $F_1, \dots, F_t$ of $F(R^q)$, and hence of $F(R)$, each
    containing a petal of $R^q$. As $R$ maps each $F_j$ to some component of
    $F(R)$ which makes an angle $2\pi/t$ at the origin, we see that $R$ must act
    as a permutation of the set $\{F_1, \dots, F_t\}$. Further, as $a =
    \exp(2\pi i r/q)$ with $(r, q) = 1$, we find (from
    Theorem~\ref{thm:the_petal_theorem} applied to $R^q$) that $R$ (viewed as a
    permutation of the $F_j$) is a composition of, say, $k$ disjoint cycles each
    of length $q$, and if follows that $t = kq$. Thus, given $R$ in the form
    \eqref{eq:taylor_expansion_for_the_general_petal_theorem}, there is an
    integer $k$ such that $R$ has $kq$ petals at the origin, these dividing
    naturally into $k$ sets of $q$ petals such that $R$ acts as a cycle of
    length $q$ on each such set. We shall not give a formal statement of this as
    it is a special case of our final version of the Petal Theorem which follows
    very shortly.
\end{remark}

Observe that if $R$ is given by
\eqref{eq:taylor_expansion_for_the_general_petal_theorem}, then
\begin{equation*}
    R^n(z) = a^n z + \cdots,
\end{equation*}
so if $q$ does not divide $n$, then $R^n$ has only one fixed point at the
origin. If $q$ does divide $n$, say $n = mq$, then
\begin{equation*}
    R^q(z) = z + cz^{t+1} + \cdots
\end{equation*}
so
\begin{align*}
    R^n(z) &= (R^q)^m(z) \\
           &= z + mcz^{t+1} + \cdots
\end{align*}
and as $R^n$ has $t + 1$ ($= kq + 1$) fixed points at the origin.

We turn now to the final and most comprehensive version of the Petal Theorem.
Let $\{\zeta_1, \dots, \zeta_m\}$ be a rationally indifferent cycle of $R$ and
write
\begin{equation*}
    \zeta_{n+i} = R^n(\zeta_i)
\end{equation*}
for all $n$, $n \geq 1$, so that $\zeta_i = \zeta_j$ if and only if $i \equiv j
\mod m$. Essentially the only difference between this case and the previous one
is that now, $R$ maps each component of $F(R)$ that contains a petal at
$\zeta_j$ to some other component containing a petal at $\zeta_{j+1}$.

Of course, the previous version applies to the map $R^m$ at each point $\zeta_j$
of the cycle, so we now put $S = R^m$ and suppose that
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:taylor_expansion_of_m_iterate_for_the_general_petal_theorem}
    S(z) &= R^m(z) \\
         &= \zeta_j + \exp\left(\frac{2\pi i r}{q}\right) (z - \zeta_j) + b(z - \zeta_j)^{p+1} + \cdots,
\end{align}
where $(r, q) = 1$. Note that the coefficient $\exp(2\pi i r/q)$ is independent
of the choice of $j$ here as it is the multiplier of the cycle. In keeping with
the previous case, we also assume that
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:taylor_expansion_of_mq_iterate_for_the_general_petal_theorem}
    S^q(z) &= R^{mq}(z) \\
         &= \zeta_j + (z - \zeta_j) + c(z - \zeta_j)^{t+1} + \cdots,
\end{align}
where (as before, but using $S$ instead of $R$) $t = kq$ for some $k$. Note that
$S^q$ acting near $\zeta_j$ is conjugate (by the branch of $R^{-1}$ which maps
$\zeta_{j+1}$ to $\zeta_j$) to $R S^q R^{-1}$ ($= S^q$) acting near
$\zeta_{j+1}$, and so $S^q$ has the same number of fixed points at $\zeta_j$ as
it has at $\zeta_{j+1}$: this proves that $t$ in
\eqref{eq:taylor_expansion_of_mq_iterate_for_the_general_petal_theorem}, and
hence $k$ also, is independent of the choice of $\zeta_j$.

Now $S$ has $qk$ ($=t$) petals at each $\zeta_j$, hence associated with the
cycle $\{\zeta_1, \dots, \zeta_m\}$ are $mkq$ components $F_1, \dots, F_{mkq}$
of $F(R)$. The action of $R$ is to map $F_1$, say, with a petal at $\zeta_1$, to
$F_2$, say with a petal at $\zeta_2$, then $F_2$ to $F_3$ with a petal at
$\zeta_3$, and so on until $F_{m+1}$ ($=\zeta_1$). However, $F_{m+1} \neq F_1$
(unless $q = 1$) for $S$ maps $F_1$ to $F_{m+1}$. In this way, $R$ acts as a
permutation of $\{F_1, \dots, F_{mkq}\}$ as the product of $k$ cycles each of
length $mq$. Thus, we have the next (and final) version of the Petal Theorem.

\begin{theorem}[The Petal Theorem for cycles]
    \label{thm:the_petal_theorem_for_cycles}
    Let $\{\zeta_1, \dots, \zeta_m\}$ be a rationally indifferent cycle for $R$,
    and let the multiplier of $R^m$ at each point of the cycle be $\exp(2\pi i
    r/q)$ where $(r, q) = 1$. Then there exists an integer $k$, and $mkq$
    distinct components $F_1, \dots, F_{mkq}$ of $F(R)$ such that at each
    $\zeta_j$ there are exactly $kq$ of these components containing a petal of
    angle $2\pi/kq$ at $\zeta_j$. Further, $R$ acts as a permutation $\tau$ on
    $\{F_1, \dots, F_{mkq}\}$, where $\tau$ is a composition of $k$ disjoint
    cycles of length $mq$, and a petal based at $\zeta_j$ maps under $R$ to a
    petal based at $\zeta_{j+1}$.
\end{theorem}

More information can be found in
section~\ref{sec:the_Julia_set_and_periodic_points}, especially in
Theorem~\ref{thm:tau_is_invarian_under_analytic_conjugation} and \eqref{eq:taylor_expansion_for_conjugate_of_analytic_function_with_fixed_point_at_0_and_multiplier_1}.

We end with a count of the number of fixed points of $R$ in the cycle described
in the Petal Theorem: this will be needed in section~\ref{sec:local_conjugacy},
and it is convenient to prove it here while the relevant ideas are still fresh
in the reader's mind. We recall that $q \mid n$ means that $q$ divides $n$,
while $q \nmid n$ means that it does not.

\begin{theorem}[Fixed points of $R^n$ at $\zeta_j$]
    \label{thm:fixed_points_of_Rn_at_zeta_j}
    Suppose that the hypotheses of
    Theorem~\ref{thm:the_petal_theorem_for_cycles} and also
    \eqref{eq:taylor_expansion_of_m_iterate_for_the_general_petal_theorem},
    \eqref{eq:taylor_expansion_of_mq_iterate_for_the_general_petal_theorem}
    hold. 
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item If $m \nmid n$, then $R^n$ has no fixed points at $\zeta_j$.
        \item If $m \mid n$ but $mq \nmid n$, then $R^n$ has one fixed point at
        $\zeta_j$.
        \item If $mq \mid n$, then $R^n$ has $t+1$ fixed points at $\zeta_j$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[132]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

% --- IRRATIONALLY INDIFFERENT CYCLES IN F -------------------------------------
\section{Irrationally Indifferent Cycles in $F$}
\label{sec:irrationally_indifferent_cycles_in_F}

We recall the following definition.

\begin{definition}[Linearizable map]
    An analytic map $f$ is \emph{linearizable} near a fixed point $\zeta$ if $f$
    is conjugate to
    \begin{equation*}
        f_0: z \mapsto \zeta + (z - \zeta) f'(\zeta)
    \end{equation*}
    in some neighborhood of $\zeta$: explicitly, if there is some neighborhood
    $N$ of $\zeta$, and some map $g$ which fixed $\zeta$, such that $f$ is
    analytic on $N$, $g$ is analytic and injective in $N \cup f(N)$, and $g f
    g^{-1} = f_0$ on $g(N)$.
\end{definition}

We have seen that a rational map $R$ is linearizable near each attracting, and
each repelling, fixed point. Near an indifferent fixed point $\zeta$, $R$ is or
is not linearizable depending \emph{only} on whether $\zeta$ is in $F$ or $J$,
and this is our next result.

\begin{theorem}[$R$ linearizable near $\zeta$ indifferent $\iff \zeta \in F$]
    \label{thm:R_linearizable_near_indifferent_fixed_points_in_F_not_in_J}
    Let $\zeta$ be an indifferent fixed point of a rational map $R$. Then $R$ is
    linearizable near $\zeta$ if and only if $\zeta$ lies in $F(R)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[133]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

With reference to
Theorem~\ref{thm:R_linearizable_near_indifferent_fixed_points_in_F_not_in_J}, we
know that there can be indifferent fixed points of $R$ in $J(R)$ (for example,
rationally indifferent fixed points), and we shall soon see that there can be
indifferent fixed points in $F(R)$. Ignoring (for the moment) this question of
existence, we proceed to characterize the indifferent fixed points in the Fatou
set by a method which gives an alternative proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:R_linearizable_near_indifferent_fixed_points_in_F_not_in_J}. We
prove

\begin{theorem}[Fatou components containing indifferent fixed points are Siegel discs]
    \label{thm:Fatou_components_containing_indifferent_fixed_points_are_Siegel_discs}
    Let $R$ be a rational map of degree $d$, $d \geq 2$, and suppose that
    $\zeta$ is an indifferent fixed point lying in some component $F_0$ of
    $F(R)$. Then $F_0$ is simply connected, and $R: F_0 \to F_0$ is analytically
    conjugate to a rotation of infinite order of the unit disc $\Delta$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[134--136]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Siegel disc]
    Any component $F_0$ of this type is called a \emph{Siegel disc}.
\end{definition}

Siegel discs are named after C.L. Siegel who, in 1941, was the first to
establish their existence (see \cite{Siegel1942} and \cite{Siegel1971}). We
shall verify their existence here, and for this we need the following two
results.

\begin{theorem}[Condition for an analytic funtion to be linearizable]
    \label{thm:condition_for_an_analytic_function_to_be_linearizable}
    Suppose that $|\alpha| = 1$, and that for some positive $M$ and $k$, and all
    positive integers $n$,
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:condition_for_an_analytic_function_to_be_linearizable}
        \frac{1}{|\alpha^n - 1|} \leq M n^k.
    \end{equation}
    Then any analytic function $f$ with Taylor expansion
    \begin{equation*}
        f(z) = \alpha z + a_2 z^2 + \cdots
    \end{equation*}
    is linearizable near the origin.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[136--141]{Beardon1991}.

    The following lemmas are used in the proof.

    Lemma[Action Scheme of Conjugating Maps]: The maps $\varphi$, $f$ and
    $\varphi^{-1}$ act according to the scheme
    \begin{equation*}
        D_4 \xrightarrow{\varphi} D_3 \xrightarrow{f} D_2 \xrightarrow{\varphi^{-1}} D_1
    \end{equation*}

    Proof: \cite[139]{Beardon1991}.

    Lemma[Derivative Bound for G on $D_5$]: On $D_5$, $|G'(z)| \leq 2C\delta^2 / \theta^{k+2}$.

    Proof: \cite[139]{Beardon1991}.

    Or see Chapter 3 of \cite{Siegel1971} or the original proof in
    \cite{Siegel1942}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Measure of the set of $\alpha$'s that satisfy the condition for an analytic function to be linearizable]
    \label{thm:measure_of_the_set_of_alphas}
    Let $S$ be the set of numbers $\alpha$ which satisfy $|\alpha| = 1$ and
    \eqref{eq:condition_for_an_analytic_function_to_be_linearizable} for some
    $M$ and $k$, and all $n$. Then $S$ has measure $2 \pi$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[141--142]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

These imply the existence of Siegel discs, for
Theorem~\ref{thm:measure_of_the_set_of_alphas} shows that there is some $\alpha$
in $S$, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:condition_for_an_analytic_function_to_be_linearizable} then
implies that $P: z \mapsto \alpha z + z^2$ is linearizable near the origin. It
follows from
Theorem~\ref{thm:R_linearizable_near_indifferent_fixed_points_in_F_not_in_J}
that $0 \in F(P)$ and finally, by
Theorem~\ref{thm:Fatou_components_containing_indifferent_fixed_points_are_Siegel_discs},
that the origin is the center of a Siegel disc for $P$. Much work has been done
to find necessary and sufficient conditions on $\alpha$ for $f$ to be
linearizable, and we refer the reader to \cite{Herman1986} for more details.

% --- IRRATIONALLY INDIFFERENT CYCLES IN J -------------------------------------
\section{Irrationally Indifferent Cycles in $J$}
\label{sec:irrationally_indifferent_cycles_in_J}

Our earlier results give us a clear understanding of the dynamics of the
iterates $R^n$ in the vicinity of all attracting, repelling, and rationally
indifferent cycles, and also irrationally indifferent cycles in $F$, and we
shall consider super-attracting cycles in Theorem~\ref{thm:simple_local_analytic_conjugate_for_superattracting_fixed_point}. The one
result in these sections shows that the remaining possibility, namely
irrationally indifferent cycles in $J$, can occur although the geometry and
dynamics of the iterates in a neighborhood of these cycles seems not to be
understood at all. We prove

\begin{theorem}[Condition for existence of irrationally indifferent fixed points in J for polynomials]
    \label{thm:condition_for_irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point_in_J_of_P}
    Suppose that $P(z) = \alpha z + \cdots + z^d$, where $d \geq 2$ and
    $|\alpha| = 1$ but $\alpha$ is not a root of unity. If
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:condition_for_irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point_in_J_of_P}
        |\alpha^n - 1| \leq \left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{d^n - 1}
    \end{equation}
    for infinitely many $n$, then the origin is an irrationally indifferent
    fixed point of $P$ that lies in $J(P)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[143]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Compare conditions on $\alpha$]
    The reader should compare \eqref{eq:condition_for_irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point_in_J_of_P} with
    \eqref{eq:condition_for_an_analytic_function_to_be_linearizable}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[$\alpha$'s verifying \eqref{eq:condition_for_irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point_in_J_of_P} exist]
    Of course,
    Theorem~\ref{thm:condition_for_irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point_in_J_of_P}
    does not establish the existence of irrationally indifferent fixed points in
    $J$, for we have yet to show that number $\alpha$ satisfying
    \eqref{eq:condition_for_irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point_in_J_of_P} do
    exist. In fact, it is easy to see that such numbers $\alpha$ are dense in the
    unit circle, and we begin by showing that if $\alpha = \exp(2\pi i \theta)$,
    then the condition
    \eqref{eq:condition_for_irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point_in_J_of_P} is
    closely related to the approximation of $\theta$ by rational numbers.

    Now for any positive integers $m$ and $n$, we have
    \begin{align*}
        |\alpha^n - 1| &= |e^{n\pi i \theta} - e^{-n\pi i \theta}| \\
                    &= 2|\sin(n\pi\theta)| \\
                    &= 2|\sin\pi(n\theta - m)| \\
                    &\leq 2\pi |n\theta - m|.
    \end{align*}
    It follows that
    \eqref{eq:condition_for_irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point_in_J_of_P}
    holds if for infinitely many $n$ there is some $m$ (depending on $n$) such
    that
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:condition_for_existence_of_alpha_verifying_the_condition_for_irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point_in_J_of_P}
        \left|\theta - \frac{m}{n}\right| \leq \frac{1}{n^{N+2}},
    \end{equation}
    for then (with $n > 2\pi$)
    \begin{equation*}
        |\alpha^n - 1| \leq 2\pi n \left|\theta - \frac{m}{n}\right| \leq \frac{1}{n^{N}}.
    \end{equation*}

    The problem, then, reduces to showing that the set of $\theta$ satisfying
    \eqref{eq:condition_for_existence_of_alpha_verifying_the_condition_for_irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point_in_J_of_P}
    is dense in $\R$. Now each real number $x$ has a continued fraction
    expansion and the convergents of the continued fractions are particularly
    good rational approximations to $x$. By defining $\theta$ in terms of a
    specified continued fraction expansion, we can construct many $\theta$
    satisfying
    \eqref{eq:condition_for_existence_of_alpha_verifying_the_condition_for_irrationally_indifferent_fixed_point_in_J_of_P},
    and it is apparent from the construction that the set of such $\theta$ is
    dense in $\R$. A self-contained argument for the construction is given in
    the exercises \cite{Beardon1991}.
\end{remark}

\section{The Julia Set and Periodic Points}
\label{sec:the_Julia_set_and_periodic_points}

We are now in a position to relate the Julia set to periodic points. First, we
prove

\begin{theorem}[$J = \derived{\{\text{periodic points}\}}$]
    \label{thm:J_is_the_derived_set_of_the_periodic_points}
    Let $R$ be a rational map of degree $d$, where $d \geq 2$. Then $J$ is the
    derived set of the periodic points of $R$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[148--149]{Beardon1991}.

    In the proof Lemma~\ref{lem:components_of_F_have_at_most_one_periodic_point}
    is used to prove that the derived set of the periodic points of $R$ is
    contained in $J$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Recall of definition of accumulation point]
    We recall that $z$ is in the derived set of $E$ if and only if there are
    \emph{distinct} points $z_n$ in $E$ such that $z_n \to z$.
\end{remark}

Later, we shall see that $R$ has at most $2d - 2$ non-repelling cycles, and with
this Theorem~\ref{thm:J_is_the_derived_set_of_the_periodic_points} implies that
$J$ is the derived set of the repelling cycles. As each repelling cycles lies in
$J$ (and anticipating the result in section~9.6 of \cite{Beardon1991}), this
yields

\begin{theorem}[$J = \closure{\{\text{repelling periodic points}\}}$]
    $J$ is the closure of the repelling periodic points of $R$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{lemma}[Components of $F$ have at most one periodic point]
    \label{lem:components_of_F_have_at_most_one_periodic_point}
    Any component of $F$ contains at most one periodic point of $R$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[149]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

We end this section with the following extension of
Theorem~\ref{thm:union_of_iterates_of_an_open_set_that_meet_J_explode_to_ExtC_minus_exceptional_points}

\begin{theorem}[$W$ domain and $W \cap J \neq \emptyset \implies R^n(W)$ contains any compact set that doesn't have exceptional points]
    Let $R$ be a rational map of degree $d$, where $d \geq 2$, let $W$ be a
    domain that meets $J$, and let $K$ be a compact set which contains no
    exceptional points of $R$. Then for all sufficiently large $n$, $R^n(W)
    \subset K$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[149--150]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

\section{Local Conjugacy}
\label{sec:local_conjugacy}

Let
\begin{equation*}
    f(z) = a_1 z + a_2 z^2 + \cdots
\end{equation*}
be a power series, analytic near the origin. In this final section of the
chapter, we comment briefly on the possible forms that the local conjugates $g f
g^{-1}$ of $f$ can take. We shall assume that $g(0) = 0$, and we shall discuss
\emph{analytic conjugacy} (when $g$ is analytic), \emph{topological conjugacy}
(when $g$ is a homeomorphism), and \emph{formal conjugacy} (when $g$ is a formal
power series).

We have already discussed analytic conjugacy when $|a_1| \neq 0, 1$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:koenigs_local_linearization}). The situation when $a_1 = 0$ is
straightforward, and the most optimistic guess is that in this case $f$ is
conjugate to the first non-trivial term of its Taylor expansion, say to $a_q
z^q$ where $q \geq 2$. This is correct, but as $q \geq 2$, the two maps $z
\mapsto a_q z^q$ and $z \mapsto z^q$ are conjugate, and so we can state this
result in the following form

\begin{theorem}[Simple local analytic conjugate for super-attracting fixed point]
    \label{thm:simple_local_analytic_conjugate_for_superattracting_fixed_point}
    Suppose that
    \begin{equation*}
        f(z) = b_0 z^q + b_1 z^{q+1} + \cdots
    \end{equation*}
    is analytic near the origin, and that $b_0 \neq 0$, $q \geq 2$. Then there
    exists a unique function $g$, analytic near the origin, such that:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item $g(0) = 0$, $g'(0) = 1$; and
        \item for all $z$ near the origin, $g f g^{-1}(z) = z^q$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[151--152]{Beardon1991}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Principal root bound]
    Suppose that $n \geq 2$, let $W = \{w \colon |w-1| < 1/2\}$, and let
    $z^{1/n}$ denote the principal branch of the $n$-th root of $z$ in $W$. Then
    \begin{equation*}
        \left|z^{\frac{1}{n}} - 1\right| < 2\frac{|z - 1|}{n} < \frac{1}{n}.
    \end{equation*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[151]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

We shall now briefly consider the local analytic conjugates of $f$ when $a_1 =
1$. In this case, $f$ has the form
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:taylor_expansion_for_analytic_function_with_fixed_point_at_0_and_multiplier_1}
    f(z) = z(1 + \alpha_p z^p + \alpha_{p+1} z^{p+1} + \cdots)
\end{equation}
and by the Residue Theorem, the function $\tau(f)$ defined by
\begin{equation*}
    \tau(f) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{|z|=r} \frac{1}{f(z) - z} \D z
\end{equation*}
is independent of $r$ for $r < r_0$, say. Further, if we expand $(f(z) -
z)^{-1}$ in a Laurent series about the origin, we see that $\tau(f)$ is of the
form
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{P(\alpha_p, \dots, \alpha_{2p})}{(\alpha_p)^{p+1}}
\end{equation*}
for some polynomial $P$: for example, if $p = 1$, then
\begin{equation*}
    \tau(f) = -\frac{\alpha_2}{\alpha_1^2}.
\end{equation*}
We now prove that the function $\tau$ is invariant under analytic conjugation.

\begin{theorem}[$\tau$ is invariant under analytic conjugation]
    \label{thm:tau_is_invarian_under_analytic_conjugation}
    If $f$ is of the form
    \eqref{eq:taylor_expansion_for_analytic_function_with_fixed_point_at_0_and_multiplier_1},
    and is analytically conjugate to $g$ near the origin, then $\tau(f) =
    \tau(g)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[153--154]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

As an application of
Theorem~\ref{thm:tau_is_invarian_under_analytic_conjugation}, recall that if $f$
is of the form
\eqref{eq:taylor_expansion_for_analytic_function_with_fixed_point_at_0_and_multiplier_1},
then $f$ is locally conjugate to a function
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:taylor_expansion_for_conjugate_of_analytic_function_with_fixed_point_at_0_and_multiplier_1}
    z \mapsto z(1 + z^p + Az^{2p} + O(z^{2p+1}))
\end{equation}
(Theorem~\ref{thm:condition_relaxation_for_the_petal_theorem}), and in this
case, a simple calculation gives
\begin{equation*}
    \tau(f) = -A.
\end{equation*}

Next, we have

\begin{theorem}[Elimination of intermediate terms]
    Suppose that $f$ is given by
    \eqref{eq:taylor_expansion_for_analytic_function_with_fixed_point_at_0_and_multiplier_1}:
    then for any integer $q$ with $q > 2p$, $f$ is analytically conjugate to
    some function
    \begin{equation*}
        z \mapsto z(1 + z^p + A z^{2p} + O(z^q)).
    \end{equation*}
\end{theorem}

The proof is essentially the same as that for
Theorem~\ref{thm:condition_relaxation_for_the_petal_theorem}, and we omit the
details. By contrast, we also have

\begin{theorem}[Analytic Conjugation to a Linear Map Modulo $z^N$]
    Suppose that $f$ has a Taylor expansion
    \begin{equation*}
        f(z) = a_1 z + a_2 z^2 + \cdots,
    \end{equation*}
    where $a_1$ is not a root of unity. Then given any positive integer $N$,
    there is some function $g$, analytic near and vanishing at the origin, such
    that near the origin
    \begin{equation*}
        g^{-1} f g(z) = a_1 z + O(z^N).
    \end{equation*}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[154]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

We turn now to discuss topological conjugacy and here, of course, the conjugacy
classes are much larger.

\begin{remark}[Fixed points are preserved under topological conjugacy]
    First, observe that the dynamics of the iterates $f^n$ near an attracting
    fixed point is invariant under topological conjugacy, for if $f^n(z) \to 0$
    and $F = h f h^{-1}$, then $F^n(hz) \to 0$ also, but in this case we cannot
    talk of the multiplier. In fact, given any two functions $f$ and $g$ (not
    necessarily analytic) which are orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of
    some neighborhood of the origin into itself such that $|f(z)| < |z|$ and
    $|g(z)| < |z|$, then $f$ and $g$ are topologically conjugate
    \cite{Sternberg1957}.
\end{remark}

In the case of rationally indifferent fixed points, we have

\begin{theorem}[Conjugation for finite order iterates]
    Suppose that
    \begin{equation*}
        f(z) = a_1 z + a_2 z^2 + \cdots,
    \end{equation*}
    where $a_1 = \exp(2\pi i p / q)$, $(p, q) = 1$, and also that the iterate
    $f^n$ is the identity. Then there is some integer $k$ such that near the
    origin, $f$ is analytically conjugate to
    \begin{equation*}
        z \mapsto a_1 z (1 + z^{kq} + \cdots),
    \end{equation*}
    and topologically conjugate to $z \mapsto z(1 + z^{kq})$.
\end{theorem}

The reader is referred to \cite{Camacho1978} for the details.

Finally, we comment briefly on the question of formal conjugacy, and we mention
just one result which sheds more light on the work in this section.

\begin{theorem}[Voronin's formal conjugacy classification]
    Any formal power series about the origin is formally conjugate to one and
    only one formal series of the form $z(1 + z^p + A z^{2p})$.
\end{theorem}

The proof is by repeated applications of
Theorem~\ref{thm:tau_is_invarian_under_analytic_conjugation}, and we refer the
reader to \cite{Voronin1981} for more details.
