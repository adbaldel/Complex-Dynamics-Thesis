\chapter{The No Wandering Domains Theorem}
\label{chp:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}

This chapter is devoted to the proof of Sullivan's important theorem, namely
that for any component $\Omega$ of the Fatou set of $R$, the sequence $\Omega,
R(\Omega), R^2(\Omega), \dots$ is eventually periodic.

% --- SECTION ------------------------------------------------------------------
% --- THE NO WANDERING DOMAINS THEOREM -----------------------------------------
\section{The No Wandering Domains Theorem}
\label{sec:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}

Consider the sequence
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Fatou_component_iterates}
    \Omega, R(\Omega), R^2(\Omega), \dots, R^n(\Omega), \dots
\end{equation}
of successive images of a component $\Omega$ of the Fatou set of a rational map
$R$. This chapter is devoted to the proof that this sequence is eventually
periodic (the only alternative being that the regions in
\eqref{eq:Fatou_component_iterates} are pairwise disjoint). We begin with the
terminology which enables us to refer briefly to each possibility and, as usual,
$R$ is a rational map with $\deg(R) \geq 2$.

\begin{definition}[Types of components of $F$]
    A component $\Omega$ of the Fatou set $F(R)$ is:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
        \item \emph{periodic} if for some positive integer $n$, $R^n(\Omega) =
        \Omega$;
        \item \emph{eventually periodic} if for some positive integer $m$,
        $R^m(\Omega)$ is periodic; and
        \item \emph{wandering} if the sets $R^n(\Omega)$, $n \geq 0$, are
        pairwise disjoint.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

A wandering component of $F(R)$ is called a \emph{wandering domain} of $R$.
Fatou asked whether such domains can exist and in 1985, \cite{Sullivan1985},
Sullivan proved that they cannot (for an alternative proof, see
\cite{Bers1987}).

\begin{theorem}[The No Wandering Domains Theorem]
    \label{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}
    Every component of the Fatou set of a rational map is eventually periodic.
\end{theorem}

Sullivan's result is probably the most significant advance made in this subject
in recent times, and we shall devote this entire chapter to its proof. It
implies, for example, that the long-term dynamic of a rational map $R$ on its
Fatou set $F$ is the action of $R$ on the periodic components of $F$, and as a
periodic component of $F$ is forward invariant under some $R^m$, we have already
analyzed this situation in Chapter~\ref{chp:forward_invariant_components}.
Further important applications arise by combining it with the results on
critical points in Chapter~9 \cite{Beardon1991}, for periodic components imply
the existence of critical points (see
Theorems~\ref{thm:F0_attracting_component_implies_F0_contains_a_critical_point}
and \ref{thm:F0_parabolic_component_implies_F0_contains_a_critical_point}), and
from the Riemann-Hurwitz relation we know that there are at most $2d - 2$
critical points. The rest of this chapter is concerned only with the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}, and the reader who is
primarily interested in applications can now pass safely on to Chapter~9.

Although we shall not consider entire functions in this text, we remark that
wandering domains can exist for these: see references in
\cite[177]{Beardon1991}.

% --- A PRELIMINARY RESULT -----------------------------------------------------
\section{A Preliminary Result}
\label{sec:a_preliminary_result}

The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem} will be by
contradiction so we suppose now that $R$ does have a wandering domain. In this
section we shall derive a consequence of this assumption which enables us to
simplify Sullivan's original proof by restricting ourselves to simply connected
components of $F(R)$: of course, this only contributes towards the required
contradiction, and it will have no significance at all once
Theorem~\ref{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem} has been proved. We prove

\begin{lemma}[Properties of wandering domains of $R$]
    \label{lem:properties_of_wandering_domains_of_R}
    Suppose that $R$ has a wandering domain. Then for some component $W$ of
    $F(R)$, the components
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:Fatou_wandering_component_iterates}
        W, R(W), R^2(W), \dots, R^n(W), \dots
    \end{equation}
    of $F(R)$ are pairwise disjoint, simply connected, and contain no critical
    points of $R$. In particular, each is mapped by $R$ homeomorphically onto
    the next.
\end{lemma}

The heart of the argument lies in showing that the existence of a wandering
domain implies the existence of a simply connected wandering domain. We use
$\diameter[E]$ to denote the diameter of a set $E$ with respect to the spherical
metric, and as a first step, we prove

\begin{lemma}[Compact subsets of wandering disappear]
    \label{lem:compact_subsets_of_wandering_domains_disappear}
    Suppose that $W$ is a wandering domain. Then for any compact subset $K$ of
    $W$, $\diameter[R^n(K)] \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Suppose that this is false: then there is some compact subset $K$ of $W$,
    some positive $\varepsilon$, and some increasing sequence $n_j$ of integers
    such that for $j = 1, 2, \dots$,
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:diam_of_compact_subsets_of_wandering_domains_doesnt_disappear}
        \diameter[R^{n_j}(W)] \geq \varepsilon.
    \end{equation}
    As $\{R^n\}$ is normal in $W$, there is a subsequence of $R^{n_j}$ which
    converges locally uniformly on $W$ to some analytic function $g$: for
    convenience, we relabel this subsequence and so assume that $R^{n_j}$ itself
    has this property. Now if $g$ is constant, with value $\alpha$ say, on $W$,
    then $R^{n_j}$ converges uniformly to $\alpha$ on $K$ and so, for large $j$,
    $R^{n_j}(K)$ lies in a $\varepsilon/3$-neighbourhood of $\alpha$. This
    contradicts
    \eqref{eq:diam_of_compact_subsets_of_wandering_domains_doesnt_disappear},
    and we conclude that $R^{n_j}$ converges to a non-constant $g$ locally
    uniformly on $W$.

    We now take a point $\zeta$ in $W$ where $g'(\zeta) \neq 0$ and draw a small
    circle $C$ with center $\zeta$ which with its interior $D$ lies in $W$, and
    which is such that $g(z) \neq g(\zeta)$ when $z$ is on $C$. Then for $j \geq
    j_0$, say,
    \begin{equation*}
        |R^{n_j}(z) - g(z)| < \inf_{w \in C} |g(w) - g(z)| < |g(z) - g(\zeta)|
    \end{equation*}
    on $C$ and so by Rouché's Theorem, $R^{n_j}(D)$ contains the point
    $g(\zeta)$. This contradicts the fact that $W$ is a wandering domain and so
    completes the proof.
\end{proof}

We can now give the

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:properties_of_wandering_domains_of_R}]
    First, $R$ has a wandering domain, $\Omega$ say, so only a finite number of
    the sets $R^n(\Omega)$ contain critical points of $R$. Taking $N$ to be
    sufficiently large, we let $W = R^N(\Omega)$ and $W_n = R^n(W)$, so $W$ is a
    wandering domain and no $W_j$ contains any critical points of $R$. As a
    consequence of this, we have
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:Riemann_Hurwitz_relation_for_a_wandering_domain}
        \chi(W_n) = m_n \chi(W_{n+1}),
    \end{equation}
    where $R$ is an $m_n$-fold map of $W_n$ onto $W_{n+1}$
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:Riemann_Hurwitz_relation_for_related_Fatou_components}).
    Now it is only necessary to prove that $W$ ($= W_0$) is simply connected for
    then, from \eqref{eq:Riemann_Hurwitz_relation_for_related_Fatou_components},
    $m_0 \chi(W_1) = 1$; hence $W_1$ is also simply connected, $m_0 = 1$ and the
    argument then proceeds by induction in the same way to drive the simple
    connectivity of $W_n$ for all subsequent $n$.

    To complete the proof, then, we show that $W$ is simply connected, and again
    we proceed by contradiction. Given that $W$ is not simply connected, there
    is a simple closed curve, say $\gamma$, in $W$ which is not homotopically
    trivial in $W$, and we define $\gamma_n = R^n(\gamma)$. Now
    \begin{equation*}
        R^n: W \to R^n(W)
    \end{equation*}
    is a smooth unlimited covering map (for by construction, there are no
    critical points of any of the factors $R$ in the composition $R^n$), and
    this ensures that $\gamma_n$ is not homotopically trivial in $R^n(W)$, for
    if it were, the Monodromy Theorem would enable us to take the deformation of
    $\gamma_n$ into a point of $R^n(W)$ and lift it to a deformation of $\gamma$
    into a point of $W$.

    We now recall
    Theorem~\ref{thm:interior_components_mapping_theorem_for_rational_maps} and
    the $\delta$ defined in that result. By
    Lemma~\ref{lem:compact_subsets_of_wandering_domains_disappear} (with $K =
    \gamma$) there is some $m$ such that for $n \geq m$,
    \begin{equation*}
        \diameter[\gamma_n] \leq \delta.
    \end{equation*}
    Now according to
    Theorem~\ref{thm:interior_components_mapping_theorem_for_rational_maps}, if
    $n \geq m$ then $R$ maps the union of $\gamma_n$ and its interior components
    into the union of $\gamma_{n+1}$ and its interior components. Using
    Theorem~\ref{thm:analytic_maps_not_touching_af_bf_and_cf_form_a_normal_family},
    we deduce that $\{R^n \mid n \geq 1\}$ is normal in each interior component
    of $\gamma_m$ and so each such component lies in $F$. It now follows that
    the union of $\gamma_m$ and all its interior components is a connected,
    compact subset of $F(R)$ whose complement is (by
    Proposition~\ref{prp:simply_connected_iff_complement_connected}) simply
    connected. We deduce that $\gamma_m$ is homotopic to a point in $F(R)$ (see
    Exercise~8.2.1 \cite{Beardon1991}), and hence also in $W_n$, contrary to our
    earlier observation. It follows that $W$ is simply connected, and the proof
    is complete.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Note about the proof]
    When (in section~\ref{sec:the_proof_of_the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}) we
    proceed to the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}
    by contradiction, we shall assume that the (hypothetical) wandering domain
    has those properties given in
    Lemma~\ref{lem:properties_of_wandering_domains_of_R}.
\end{remark}

\section{Conformal Structures}
\label{sec:conformal_structures}

We shall need certain facts about quasiconformal maps and in order to avoid too
great a disruption to the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}, we shall adopt a rather
axiomatic view and confine ourselves to a brief discussion of the few standard
results that we need. The reader is referred to \cite{Ahlfors2006},
\cite{Lehto1987} and \cite{Lehto1973} for the proofs and the background
material.

We begin with a generalization of the Cauchy-Riemann equations. Given any
function $f$ with continuous partial derivatives in a domain $D$, we introduce
the two differential operators
\begin{equation*}
    \pbyp{f}{\overline{z}} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\pbyp{f}{x} + i \pbyp{f}{y}\right), \quad \pbyp{f}{z} = \frac{1}{2i}\left(\pbyp{f}{x} - i \pbyp{f}{y}\right)
\end{equation*}
Observe that if $f$ is analytic, then
\begin{equation*}
    \pbyp{f}{\overline{z}} = 0, \quad \pbyp{f}{z} = f'(z),
\end{equation*}
the first of these being the Cauchy-Riemann equations. 

\begin{definition}[Beltrami equation]
    Our generalization of these equations is the
    \emph{Beltrami equation}
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:Beltrami_equation}
        \pbyp{f}{\overline{z}} = \mu \pbyp{f}{z},
    \end{equation}
    where $\mu$ is some suitable complex-valued function on $D$
\end{definition}

The basic idea is that if $\mu = 0$ throughout $D$, then any sufficiently smooth
solution $f$ of \eqref{eq:Beltrami_equation} is analytic in $D$, while in the
general situation, $\mu$ is taken as a measure of the deviation of a solution
$f$ from conformality. Of course, given a function $f$ on $D$, we can use
\eqref{eq:Beltrami_equation} to define $\mu$.

\begin{definition}[Complex dilation of $f$]
    This $\mu$ (when it exists) is called the complex dilatation of $f$ and is
    denoted by $\mu_f$.
\end{definition}

An essential feature of the theory is that it is developed for a very large
class of $\mu$; most notably, $\mu$ is not required to be continuous and as a
result we have a tool of great flexibility. The functions $\mu: D \to \C$ are
required to be Lebesgue measurable and to satisfy
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:condition_for_mu_to_be_Beltrami_coefficient}
    ||\mu||_\infty < 1
\end{equation}
in $D$, so $|\mu| \leq ||\mu||_\infty$ almost everywhere (that is, except on a
set of zero area).

\begin{definition}[Beltrami coefficient]
    We shall call any such function $\mu$ a \emph{Beltrami coefficient} on $D$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Quasiconformal homeomorphism]
    Given a domain $D$ and a Beltrami coefficient $\mu$ on $D$, we say that a
    homeomorphism $f$ on $D$ is \emph{quasiconformal} with \emph{complex
    dilation} $\mu$ on $D$ if $f$ is an $L^2$ solution of
    \eqref{eq:Beltrami_equation} in $D$ (see \cite[24]{Lehto1987}).
\end{definition}

The reader need not worry about the technical nature of the definition as we
shall not mention these ideas again; however, some explanatory remarks may be
helpful.

\begin{remark}[Quasiconformal maps are differentiable]
    First, a quasiconformal map $f$ is differentiable (in the sense that its
    derivative is a linear map from $\R^2$ to itself) almost everywhere (a.e.)
    in $D$, and we only require \eqref{eq:Beltrami_equation} to be satisfied
    a.e. in $D$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Quasiconformal maps move infinitesimal circles to infinitesimal ellipses with bounded eccentricity]
    Second, the intuitive implication of \eqref{eq:Beltrami_equation} is that
    for almost all $z$, the function $f$ maps an infinitesimal circle $C$
    centered at $z$ to an infinitesimal ellipse $E$ centered at $f(z)$, where
    both the eccentricity and the orientation of $E$ are determined by $\mu(z)$.
    The significance of \eqref{eq:condition_for_mu_to_be_Beltrami_coefficient}
    is that it controls this distortion induced by $f$, and it implies that the
    eccentricity of $E$ is bounded above throughout $D$.
\end{remark}

We turn now to the basic existence and uniqueness theorem for solutions of
\eqref{eq:Beltrami_equation}; see Theorems~4.2 and 4.4 in \cite{Lehto1987}.

\begin{theorem}[Existence of quasiconformal maps with a given complex dilation]
    \label{thm:existence_of_quasiconformal_maps_with_a_given_complex_dilation}
    Let $\mu$ be a Beltrami coefficient on a domain $D$ on the complex sphere.
    Then:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item
        \label{itm:existence_of_quasiconformal_maps_with_a_given_complex_dilation}
        there exists a quasiconformal map with complex dilation $\mu$ on $D$;
        and
        \item \label{itm:transition_between_coordinate_charts_in_analytic} if
        $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are two such maps, then $\varphi\psi^{-1}$ is
        analytic.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Taking $\mu$ to be identically zero and $\psi$ to be the identity we obtain

\begin{corollary}[Quasiconformal with dilation $0 \implies$ conformal]
    If $\varphi$ is quasiconformal with dilation $0$ on $D$, then it is
    conformal on $D$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Measurable Riemann Mapping Theorem]
    Given a simply connected domain $D$ (conformally equivalent to the unit disc
    $\Delta$), and a Beltrami coefficient $\mu$ on $D$, then there is a
    quasiconformal map $\varphi$ of $D$ onto $\Delta$ with dilation $\mu$ almost
    everywhere in $D$: this is the \emph{Measurable Riemann Mapping Theorem} for
    the case when $\mu$ is identically zero is the classical Riemann Mapping
    Theorem.
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:existence_of_quasiconformal_maps_with_a_given_complex_dilation}
leads naturally to the idea that each $\mu$ on $D$ creates a Riemann surface,
namely the space $D$ together with a conformal structure on $D$ determined by
$\mu$. For a given $D$ and $\mu$, this conformal structure is defined by the
atlas $\mathcal{A}(\mu)$ (the totality of charts) consisting of the family of all
maps $\varphi: D \to \ExtC$ which are quasiconformal with dilation $\mu$ on $D$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:existence_of_quasiconformal_maps_with_a_given_complex_dilation}\ref{itm:existence_of_quasiconformal_maps_with_a_given_complex_dilation}
guarantees that each point of $D$ is in the domain of some such $\varphi$, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:existence_of_quasiconformal_maps_with_a_given_complex_dilation}\ref{itm:transition_between_coordinate_charts_in_analytic}
guarantees that the transition map between two different co-ordinate charts is
analytic; thus, $\mathcal{A}(\mu)$ defines a conformal structure on $D$ and so
converts $D$ into a Riemann surface.

\begin{definition}[$\mu$-conformal structure]
    We shall denote this Riemann surface by $D[\mu]$, and we call the structure
    the \emph{$\mu$-conformal structure} on $D$.
\end{definition}

Of course, if $\mu \equiv 0$, then $D[\mu]$ is simply $D$ with the usual
structure as a subdomain of $\ExtC$, and we usually use $D$ rather than $D[0]$;
however, where there may be some gain in clarity, we shall use $D[0]$.

\begin{definition}[$\mu$-analytic]
    Given the Riemann surface $D[\mu]$, we say that the map
    \begin{equation*}
        f: D \to \ExtC
    \end{equation*}
    is $\mu$-analytic if it is an analytic map between the Riemann surfaces
    $D[\mu]$ and $\ExtC$ (with the usual structure).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$\mu$-conformal]
    If, in addition, $f$ is a homeomorphism, we say that it is $\mu$-conformal
    on $D$.
\end{definition}

Of course, we retain the usual usage of \emph{analytic} and \emph{conformal}
when $\mu$ is identically zero.

\begin{remark}[Analyticity is preserved under composition]
    Note that if $f$ is $\mu$-analytic on $D$, and $g$ is analytic on $f(D)$,
    then (as the composition of analytic maps is analytic), $fg$ is
    $\mu$-analytic on $D$. 
\end{remark}

Quite generally, a homeomorphism $\varphi$ form an open subset of a Riemann
surface $\mathcal{R}$ to $\ExtC$ is analytic if and only if it is a chart in the
maximal atlas for $\mathcal{R}$; thus we record the different points of view in

\begin{lemma}[Characterizations of $\mu$-conformal]
    \label{lem:characterizations_of_muconformal}
    Let $\varphi$ be a homeomorphism defined on $D$. Then the following are
    equivalent:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item $\varphi$ is $\mu$-conformal on $D$;
        \item $\varphi$ is a chart for $D[\mu]$;
        \item $\varphi: D[\mu] \to \ExtC[0]$ is analytic
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

Our last task in this section is to formulate the transfer of a conformal
structure from one domain to another in terms of complex dilatations. If $g$ is
a bijection of a Riemann surface $X$ onto a set $Y$, then $g$ can be used to
transfer the conformal  structure on $X$ to $Y$ in a natural, trivial, and
unique, way so that $g: X \to Y$ is analytic. We need to express this transfer
of structure in terms of Beltrami coefficients and we prove

\begin{lemma}[$\mu$-conformal structure transfer via Beltrami coefficients]
    \label{lem:muconformal_structure_transfer}
    Suppose that $\mu$ and $\nu$ are Beltrami coefficients on the domains $U$
    and $V$ respectively and let $g$ be an analytic map of $U$ onto $V$. Then:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item \label{itm:muconformal_structure_transfer_1} $g: U[\mu] \to
        V[\nu]$ is analytic; if and only if
        \item \label{itm:muconformal_structure_transfer_2} $\nu(gz) =
        [g'(z)/\overline{g'(z)}]\mu(z)$ a.e. in $U$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    See \cite[182]{Beardon1991}.
\end{proof}

We shall use Lemma~\ref{lem:muconformal_structure_transfer} to transfer
structure in two different ways. First, if $g$ is an analytic homeomorphism from
$U$ to $V$, and if $\mu$ is given on $U$, then we can define $\nu$ by
\ref{itm:muconformal_structure_transfer_2} and, as a result,
\ref{itm:muconformal_structure_transfer_1} will hold: thus we have transferred
the structure from $U$ to $V$. If we no longer assume $g$ is injective, this
process may not be valid as \ref{itm:muconformal_structure_transfer_2} may
provide conflicting definitions of $\nu$ at some point of $V$. However, in this
case, if $\nu$ is given, we can use \ref{itm:muconformal_structure_transfer_2}
to define $\mu$ and again force \ref{itm:muconformal_structure_transfer_1} to be
true: thus we have transferred the structure from $V$ to $U$. Thus, quite
generally, we can pull back a conformal structure against the action of an
analytic function so that the function remains analytic with respect to the two
new structures. We shall use both of these ideas in the next section.

% --- QUASICONFORMAL CONJUGATES OF RATIONAL MAPS -------------------------------
\section{Quasiconformal Conjugates of Rational Maps}
\label{sec:quasiconformal_conjugates_of_rational_maps}

In this section we focus attention on the conjugate $\varphi R \varphi^{-1}$ of
a rational map $R$ by some $\mu$-conformal map $\varphi$ of the complex sphere
onto itself. In general $\varphi R \varphi^{-1}$ will not be analytic, and our
next result enables us to recognize when it is. We have

\begin{lemma}[Preservation of rationality via quasiconformal conjugation]
    \label{lem:preservation_of_rationality_via_quasiconformal_conjugation}
    Suppose that $R$ is a rational map, and that $\varphi$ is a $\mu$-conformal
    map of the complex sphere onto itself. Then $\varphi R \varphi^{-1}$ is
    rational if and only if
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:condition_for_preservation_of_rationality_via_quasiconformal_conjugation}
        \mu(Rz) = [R'(z)/\overline{R'(z)}]\mu(z)
    \end{equation}
    a.e. on the sphere, and when this is so, $\deg(\varphi R \varphi^{-1}) =
    \deg(R)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    By virtue of Lemma~\ref{lem:muconformal_structure_transfer}, condition
    \eqref{eq:condition_for_preservation_of_rationality_via_quasiconformal_conjugation}
    is equivalent to the statement that
    \begin{equation*}
        R: \ExtC[\mu] \to \ExtC[\mu]
    \end{equation*}
    is analytic and this, in turn, is equivalent to the statement that $\varphi
    R \varphi^{-1}$ is an analytic map of the complex sphere onto itself with
    the usual structure (for $\varphi$ is a chart of the Riemann surface
    $\ExtC[\mu]$). This proves that
    \eqref{eq:condition_for_preservation_of_rationality_via_quasiconformal_conjugation}
    holds if and only if $\varphi R \varphi^{-1}$ is analytic throughout the
    sphere, and hence rational. Moreover, $\varphi R \varphi^{-1}$ must have the
    same degree as $R$ because $\varphi$ is a homeomorphism of the sphere onto
    itself and the degree of $\varphi R \varphi^{-1}$ is given by the
    cardinality of $(\varphi R \varphi^{-1})^{-1}\{w\}$ for most points $w$.
\end{proof}

We emphasize that
\eqref{eq:condition_for_preservation_of_rationality_via_quasiconformal_conjugation}
guarantees that we can conjugate a rational map by a \emph{non-analytic}
function $\varphi$ and yet still remain analyticity.

We shall now incorporate this result into the situation in which a wandering
domain exists, and in doing so, we take a significant step forward in our proof
of Theorem~\ref{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}. We prove

\begin{lemma}[Extension of Beltrami coefficients on a wandering domain to $\ExtC$]
    \label{lem:extension_of_Beltrami_coefficients_on_a_wandering_domain_to_ExtC}
    Let $R$ be a rational map, and suppose that $W$ satisfies the conditions of
    Lemma~\ref{lem:properties_of_wandering_domains_of_R}. Then any Beltrami
    coefficient $\mu$ on $W$ extends to a Beltrami coefficient on the complex
    sphere with the property that if $\varphi$ is any $\mu$-conformal map of the
    sphere onto itself, then $\varphi R \varphi^{-1}$ is a rational map with the
    same degree as $R$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    We begin with a Beltrami coefficient $\mu$ on $W$ and our objective is to
    extend $\mu$ to the sphere so that the extension satisfies
    \eqref{eq:Beltrami_equation} a.e. on the sphere. As the sphere is the
    disjoint union of the completely invariant set $[W]$ and its (completely
    invariant) complement, say $K$, we can extend $\mu$ to $[W]$ and to $K$
    separately and then verify that \eqref{eq:Beltrami_equation} holds on each
    of these two sets. We define $\mu$ to be identically zero on $K$ so
    \eqref{eq:Beltrami_equation} is certainly satisfied there.

    The extension from $W$ to $[W]$ is carried out as follows. First, using
    Lemma~\ref{lem:properties_of_wandering_domains_of_R} and the ideas expressed
    at the end of the preceding section, we define $\mu$ on the (pairwise
    disjoint) $W_n$ ($= R^n(W)$), $n \geq 0$, by ensuring that the map
    \begin{equation*}
        R: W_n[\mu] \to W_{n+1}[\mu], \quad W_n = R^n(W),
    \end{equation*}
    is analytic. Next, we define $\mu$ on successive inverse images of the $W_n$
    by the requirement that $R$ provides an analytic map between any component
    $\Omega$ and its image under $R$ (with the $\mu$-structures): this process
    determines $\mu$ uniquely on $[W]$ and is characterized by the fact that for
    every component $\Omega$ of $[W]$, the map
    \begin{equation*}
        R: \Omega[\mu] \to R(\Omega)[\mu]
    \end{equation*}
    is analytic. As a consequence of this, the functional relation
    \eqref{eq:Beltrami_equation} holds a.e. on $[W]$ (this is simply
    Lemma~\ref{lem:muconformal_structure_transfer}) and this proves
    Lemma~\ref{lem:extension_of_Beltrami_coefficients_on_a_wandering_domain_to_ExtC}.
\end{proof}

% --- BOUNDARY VALUES OF CONJUGATE MAPS ----------------------------------------
\section{Boundary Values of Conjugate Maps}
\label{sec:boundary_values_of_conjugate_maps}

Our proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem} requires a
comparison between the boundary values of two functions which are conjugate to
each other by a conformal map, so we devote this section to this topic. Let
$\mu$ be a Beltrami coefficient on the unit disc $\Delta$, let $g$ be a
conformal map of $\Delta$ onto a simply connected domain $W$ in $\C$, and use
$g$ to transfer $\mu$ to a Beltrami coefficient $\nu$ on $W$ (as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:muconformal_structure_transfer}). Suppose now that $\Phi$ is a
$\nu$-conformal map of $W$ onto itself, and that $\Phi$ extends to a
homeomorphism of the closure of $W$ onto itself such that $\Phi \equiv \Id$ (the
identity map) on $\boundary{W}$. Then the map
\begin{equation*}
    \varphi = g^{-1} \Phi g
\end{equation*}
is a $\mu$-conformal map of $\Delta$ onto itself, and as such, it extends to a
homeomorphism of the closed disc onto itself (\cite[47]{Ahlfors2006}). Our
objective is to study the behavior of $\varphi$ on $\boundary{\Delta}$: one
approach to this problem is through the theory of prime ends, but we shall take
a different route.

When comparing maps which are conjugate by a conformal map, it is natural to try
to exploit conformal invariants and one such invariant is the hyperbolic metric.

\begin{definition}[(Hyperbolic) Displacement function of $\Phi$]
    For any homeomorphism $\Phi$ of a simply $\Omega$ onto itself, we define the
    (hyperbolic) \emph{displacement function} of $\Phi$ by
    \begin{equation*}
        z \mapsto \rho(\Phi z, z),
    \end{equation*}
    where $\rho$ is the hyperbolic metric of $\Omega$ (which exists unless
    $\Omega$ is conformally equivalent to $\C$ or $\ExtC$).
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Having a bounded displacement is preserved under conformal conjugation]
    Returning to the discussion above, the conformal equivalence $g$ is an
    isometry with respect to the hyperbolic metrics in $\Delta$ and $W$ and so
    the property of having a bounded displacement is invariant under conjugation
    by $g$: thus if $\Phi$ has a bounded displacement o $W$, then $\varphi$ has
    a bounded displacement on $\Delta$ (and conversely).
\end{remark}

This, however, implies that $\varphi = \Id$ on $\boundary{\Delta}$ for it
implies that $\varphi(z)$ lies in the hyperbolic disc $\Delta(z, d)$ with center
$z$ and some (fixed) radius $d$, and as $z$ converges to any $\zeta$ on
$\boundary{\Delta}$, the Euclidean radius of $\Delta(z, d)$ tends to zero (see
Exercise~8.5.1 \cite{Beardon1991}).

We now seek a criterion for $\Phi$ to have a bounded displacement function in
$W$, and for our purposes, it will be sufficient to assume that $\nu$ is a
Beltrami coefficient on the entire sphere, and that $\Phi$ is a $\nu$-conformal
map of the sphere onto itself. With this additional information, we can readily
obtain a criterion for $\Phi$ to have a bounded displacement function with
respect to the chordal metric on the sphere, and we can then convert this to a
criterion for $\Phi$ to have a bounded hyperbolic displacement on $W$. First, we
have

\begin{lemma}[Criterion for $\Phi$ to have a bounded displacement function with respect to the chordal metric on the sphere]
    \label{lem:criterion_for_Phi_to_have_a_bounded_displacement_function_with_respect_to_the_chordal_metric_on_the_sphere}
    For each positive $\varepsilon$ there is a positive $\delta$ such that if
    the Beltrami coefficient $\nu$ on $\ExtC$ satisfies $||\nu|| < \delta$, then
    $\sigma(Fz, z) < \varepsilon$ in $\ExtC$ for every function $F$ that is a
    $\nu$-conformal map of $\ExtC$ onto itself which fixes $0$, $1$ and
    $\infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    This is a non-sequential version of Theorem~4.6, \cite{Lehto1987}. If the
    conclusion is false there is a positive $\varepsilon$, a sequence of
    $\nu_n$-conformal maps $F_n$, and a sequence $z_n$, such that:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item
        \label{itm:property_1_of_sequence_to_prove_the_criterion_for_Phi_to_have_a_bounded_displacement_function_with_respect_to_the_chordal_metric_on_the_sphere}
        $F_n$ fixes $0$, $1$ and $\infty$;
        \item
        \label{itm:property_2_of_sequence_to_prove_the_criterion_for_Phi_to_have_a_bounded_displacement_function_with_respect_to_the_chordal_metric_on_the_sphere}
        $||\nu_n|| < 1/n$; and
        \item 
        \label{itm:property_3_of_sequence_to_prove_the_criterion_for_Phi_to_have_a_bounded_displacement_function_with_respect_to_the_chordal_metric_on_the_sphere}
        $\sigma(F_n z_n, z_n) \geq \varepsilon$.
    \end{enumerate}
    By Theorem~4.6, \cite{Lehto1987},
    \ref{itm:property_1_of_sequence_to_prove_the_criterion_for_Phi_to_have_a_bounded_displacement_function_with_respect_to_the_chordal_metric_on_the_sphere}
    and
    \ref{itm:property_2_of_sequence_to_prove_the_criterion_for_Phi_to_have_a_bounded_displacement_function_with_respect_to_the_chordal_metric_on_the_sphere}
    imply that $F_n \to 1$ uniformly on $\ExtC$, and this contradicts
    \ref{itm:property_3_of_sequence_to_prove_the_criterion_for_Phi_to_have_a_bounded_displacement_function_with_respect_to_the_chordal_metric_on_the_sphere}.
\end{proof}

Now let $\delta_0$ be the value of $\delta$ corresponding to $\varepsilon = 1/8$
in
Lemma~\ref{lem:criterion_for_Phi_to_have_a_bounded_displacement_function_with_respect_to_the_chordal_metric_on_the_sphere}.
Then, we have

\begin{lemma}[Hyperbolic displacement bound for $\nu$-conformal maps]
    \label{lem:hyperbolic_displacement_bound_for_nuconformal_maps}
    Let $\rho$ be the hyperbolic metric of a simply connected subdomain $W$ of
    $\ExtC$. If the Beltrami coefficient $\nu$ on $\ExtC$ satisfies $||\nu|| <
    \delta_0$, then $\rho(\Phi z, z) < \log(2)$ in $W$ for every function $\Phi$
    which is a $\nu$-conformal map of $\ExtC$ onto itself and which satisfies
    $\Phi(W) = W$, and $\Phi = \Id$ on $\boundary{W}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    As the statement of
    Lemma~\ref{lem:hyperbolic_displacement_bound_for_nuconformal_maps} is
    invariant under conjugation by a Möbius map, we may assume that $\infty \in
    \boundary{W}$. Now suppose that $W$, $\rho$, $\nu$ and $\Phi$ satisfy the
    hypotheses of
    Lemma~\ref{lem:hyperbolic_displacement_bound_for_nuconformal_maps} with
    $||\nu|| < \delta_0$.

    We take any $\zeta$ in $W$, let $r(\zeta)$ be the (Euclidean) distance
    between $\zeta$ and $\boundary{W}$, and suppose that this is attained at
    $\alpha$ in $\boundary{W}$. As $\boundary{W}$ is a connected set containing
    $\alpha$ and $\infty$, there is some point $\beta$ in $\boundary{W}$ such
    that
    \begin{equation*}
        |\beta - \alpha| = |\zeta - \alpha| = r(\zeta),
    \end{equation*}
    and we define the Euclidean similarity $h$ by
    \begin{equation*}
        h(z) = \frac{z - \alpha}{\beta - \alpha}.
    \end{equation*}
    The function $F$ defined by $F = h \Phi h^{-1}$ is a $\nu$-conformal map of
    $\ExtC$ onto itself, and it fixed $0$, $1$ and $\infty$ (because $\Phi$
    fixes $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\infty$); thus by
    Lemma~\ref{lem:criterion_for_Phi_to_have_a_bounded_displacement_function_with_respect_to_the_chordal_metric_on_the_sphere},
    $\sigma(F z, z) < 1/8$ on $\ExtC$. Putting $z = h(\zeta)$, we obtain
    \begin{equation*}
        \sigma(h(\Phi \zeta), h \zeta) < \frac{1}{8} < \sigma(1, 2),
    \end{equation*}
    and as $|h(\zeta)| = 1$, this implies that $|h(\Phi \zeta)| \leq 2$, and
    hence that
    \begin{align*}
        2 |h(\Phi \zeta) - h \zeta| &= \sigma(h(\Phi \zeta), h \zeta)[(1 + |h(\Phi \zeta)|^2)(1 + |h(\zeta)|^2)]^{\frac{1}{2}} \\
                                    &< \frac{1}{2}.
    \end{align*}

    Using the definition of $h$, this now yields
    \begin{equation*}
        |\Phi(\zeta) - \zeta| < \frac{r(\zeta)}{4}.
    \end{equation*}
    Now the disc $\{z \colon |z - \zeta| < r(\zeta)\}$ lies in $W$, so if
    $\rho_0$ denotes the hyperbolic metric of this disc, then, by the Comparison
    Principle for hyperbolic metrics,
    \begin{equation*}
        \rho(\Phi \zeta, \zeta) \leq \rho_0(\Phi \zeta, \zeta) \leq \log\left(\frac{1 + \frac{1}{4}}{1 - \frac{1}{4}}\right) \leq \log(2)
    \end{equation*}
    as required.
\end{proof}

In conclusion, if $\varphi: \Delta \to \Delta$ and $\Phi: W \to W$ are as given
above with $\varphi = g^{-1} \Phi \varphi$, and if $\Phi$ satisfies the
hypotheses of
Lemma~\ref{lem:hyperbolic_displacement_bound_for_nuconformal_maps}, then
$\varphi = \Id$ on $\boundary{\Delta}$.

% --- THE PROOF OF THE NO WANDERING DOMAINS THEOREM ----------------------------
\section{The Proof of The No Wandering Domains Theorem}
\label{sec:the_proof_of_the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}

In this final section of the chapter, we complete the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}. We assume that $R$ has a
wandering domain, so there exists a component $W$ of $F(R)$ with the properties
expressed in Lemma~\ref{lem:properties_of_wandering_domains_of_R}. As $W$ is
simply connected (and $J$ is infinite), there is a conformal equivalence $g$ of
$\Delta$ onto $W$. Now let $\mu$ be any Beltrami coefficient on $\Delta$. Using
$g$, we can transfer $\mu$ to $\nu$ on $W$ (as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:muconformal_structure_transfer}), and then extend $\nu$ to
$\ExtC$ as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:extension_of_Beltrami_coefficients_on_a_wandering_domain_to_ExtC}.
Solving the Beltrami equation with coefficient $\nu$ throughout the sphere, we
obtain a $\nu$-conformal map $\varphi$ of the sphere onto itself such that
$\varphi R \varphi^{-1}$ is rational (and of the same degree as $R$), and by
combining $\varphi$ with a Möbius map we may assume that $\varphi$ fixed $0$,
$1$ and $\infty$, and so is uniquely determined by $\mu$. In this way we have
created the composite map
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:composite_map_mu_nu_varphi_varphiRvarphiminus1}
    \mu \mapsto \nu \mapsto \varphi \mapsto \varphi R \varphi^{-1}
\end{equation}
of the space of Beltrami coefficients on $\Delta$ to the space of rational maps
of degree $\deg(R)$.

Roughly speaking, the space of Beltrami coefficients on $\Delta$ is infinite
dimensional while the space of rational maps of degree $\deg(R)$ is finite
dimensional, so the map $\mu \mapsto \varphi R \varphi^{-1}$ must map a large
subspace of Beltrami differentials onto a single rational function $S$. We shall
justify this later, but to maintain the flow of ideas, we formulate the result
we need as a lemma and continue with the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}. For the moment, then, we
assume the validity of

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:TO_DO_1}
    Suppose that $\eta_0 > 0$. For each $t$ in $[0, 1]$ we can construct a
    Beltrami coefficient $\mu_t$ in $\Delta$ such that:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item \label{itm:lemma_to_do_1_a} $||\mu||_\infty < \eta_0$; and
        \item \label{itm:lemma_to_do_1_b}
        \eqref{eq:composite_map_mu_nu_varphi_varphiRvarphiminus1} maps each
        $\mu_t$ to the same rational function $S$.
    \end{enumerate}
    Further, this construction can be made so that for each $z$, the map $t
    \mapsto \varphi_t(z)$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$, where $\varphi_t$ is the
    image of $\mu_t$ under the first two maps in
    \eqref{eq:composite_map_mu_nu_varphi_varphiRvarphiminus1}.
\end{lemma}

A consequence of this is that for all $t$ in $[0, 1]$,
\begin{equation*}
    \varphi_t R \varphi_t^{-1} = S = \varphi_0 R \varphi_0^{-1},
\end{equation*}
so if we put
\begin{equation*}
    \Phi_t = \varphi_0^{-1} \varphi_t
\end{equation*}
we find that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \label{itm:Phi_0_Id} $\Phi_0(z) = z$ on $\ExtC$;
    \item \label{itm:Phi_commutes_with_R} for each $t$, the map $z \mapsto
    \Phi_t(z)$ commutes with $R$;
    \item \label{itm:Phi_homeomorphism_on_ExtC} for each $t$, the map $z \mapsto
    \Phi_t(z)$ is a homeomorphism of $\ExtC$ onto itself;
    \item \label{itm:Phi_continuous_on_0_1} for each $z$, the map $t \mapsto
    \Phi_t(z)$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$.
\end{enumerate}
These properties lead to two striking facts.

\begin{lemma}[$\Phi_t$ maps components to themselves]
    \label{lem:Phi_t_maps_components_to_themselves}
    For each $t$ in $[0, 1]$, $\Phi_t = \Id$ on $J(R)$. Further, $\Phi_t$ maps
    each component of $F(R)$ onto itself.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    For $p = 1, 2, \dots$, let $F_p$ be the set of fixed points of $R^p$. By
    virtue of \ref{itm:Phi_commutes_with_R}, if $z$ is in $F_p$ then so is
    $\Phi_t(z)$; thus $\Phi_t$ maps each finite set $F_p$ into itself. Now by
    \ref{itm:Phi_continuous_on_0_1}, for each $z$ in $F_p$, the map $t \mapsto
    \Phi_t(z)$ is a continuous map of $[0, 1]$ into the discrete set $F_p$; thus
    $\Phi_t(z)$ is independent of $t$ and so, by \ref{itm:Phi_0_Id}, for all $t$
    in $[0, 1]$ and all $z$ in $F_p$, $\Phi_t(z) = z$. We deduce that for each
    $t$, $\Phi_t$ is the identity map on the union of the $F_p$, and hence by
    \ref{itm:Phi_homeomorphism_on_ExtC}, also on the closure of this union. As
    the closure of the periodic points of $R$ contains $J(R)$ (Theorem~4.2.6),
    the restriction of $\Phi_t$ to $J(R)$ is the identity. Because of this, and
    because $\Phi_t$ is a homeomorphism of the sphere onto itself, each $\Phi_t$
    must permute the components of $F(R)$. Now take any $z$ in any component
    $F_0$ of $F(R)$. By virtue of \ref{itm:Phi_0_Id} and
    \ref{itm:Phi_continuous_on_0_1}, the image of $[0, 1]$ under the map $t
    \mapsto \Phi_t(z)$ is a curve which lies in $F(R)$ and which starts at $z$.
    It follows that this curve lies entirely in $F_0$, and so for each $t$,
    $\Phi_t$ must map the arbitrary component $F_0$ into itself. This completes
    the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:Phi_t_maps_components_to_themselves}.
\end{proof}

As $\Phi_t$ maps each component $F_0$ of $F(R)$ onto itself, the homeomorphisms
$\varphi_t$ must map $W$ onto the simply connected domain $W_0$ where $W_0
(=\varphi_0(W))$ is independent of $t$. It follows that there is a conformal
equivalence $h$ of $W_0$ onto $\Delta$, and we have now reached the situation
summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:figure_8_6_1}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{beardons_book/figure_8_6_1.png}
    \caption{}
    \label{fig:figure_8_6_1}
\end{figure}

Now in the construction of $\nu$ (on $\ExtC$) from $\mu$ (on $\Delta$) in
\eqref{eq:composite_map_mu_nu_varphi_varphiRvarphiminus1} we have
$||\nu||_\infty = ||\mu||_\infty$ (see
Lemmas~\ref{lem:muconformal_structure_transfer} and
\ref{lem:preservation_of_rationality_via_quasiconformal_conjugation}), so, by
virtue of Lemma~\ref{lem:TO_DO_1}\ref{itm:lemma_to_do_1_a}, $||\nu_t||_\infty <
\eta_0$ where, of course, $\mu_t$ maps to $\nu_t$ in
\eqref{eq:composite_map_mu_nu_varphi_varphiRvarphiminus1}. By taking $\eta_0$
small enough, we can ensure that the complex dilatation of $\Phi_t
(=\varphi_0^{-1} \varphi_t)$ has $L^\infty$-norm less than $\delta_0$ as given
in Lemma~\ref{lem:hyperbolic_displacement_bound_for_nuconformal_maps}. By this
lemma, and the remarks following it, we can now conclude that the map $g^{-1}
\Phi_t g$ of $\Delta$ onto itself has bounded displacement function and so
extends to the identity on $\boundary{\Delta}$.

Now observe that each of the maps in the composition
\begin{equation*}
    \Delta[\mu_t] \xrightarrow{g} W[\nu_t] \xrightarrow{\varphi_t} W_0[0] \xrightarrow{h} \Delta[0]
\end{equation*}
is analytic, whence $\psi_t (= h \varphi_t g)$ is a $\mu_t$-conformal map of
$\Delta$ onto itself and so extends to a homeomorphism of the closed disc onto
itself. Moreover, in the open disc
\begin{equation*}
    \psi_0^{-1} \psi_t = g^{-1} \Phi_t g,
\end{equation*}
thus on $\boundary{\Delta}$, $\psi_0^{-1} \psi_t = \Id$, and so $\psi_0 =
\psi_t$.

Now let $\Psi_t$ be any $\mu_t$-conformal map of $\Delta$ onto itself. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:existence_of_quasiconformal_maps_with_a_given_complex_dilation},
$\psi_t = M_t \Psi_t$ for some Möbius automorphism $M_t$ of $\Delta$, and so we
deduce that
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mobius_automorphism_equality}
    M_t \Psi_t = M_0 \Psi_0
\end{equation}
on $\boundary{\Delta}$. In proving Lemma~\ref{lem:TO_DO_1}, we shall construct
maps $\Psi_t$ so that $\Psi_t = \Psi_0$ on some open arc of $\boundary{\Delta}$
and this will imply that $M_t = M_0$, and hence that $\Psi_t = \Psi_0$. However,
it will be clear from our construction that this is not so, and this will be the
contradiction that completes the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}.

It remains, then, to prove Lemma~\ref{lem:TO_DO_1} and to construct the maps
$\Psi_t$ with the properties used above. First, we take an integer $N$
satisfying $N > 4d + 2$ (the space of rational maps of degree $d$, $d = \deg(R)$
having $4d + 2$ real degrees of freedom). We shall now construct, for each
vector $T$ lying in the cube $[0, \epsilon_1]^N$ (where $\epsilon_1$ is some
small number yet to be chosen), a Beltrami coefficient $\mu_T$ on $\Delta$ and a
$\mu_T$-conformal map $\Psi_T$ of $\Delta$ onto itself. In fact, we shall
construct the maps $\Psi_T$ first, and then define $\mu_T$ to be their complex
dilatations.

Divide the interval $[0, 2\pi]$ into $2N$ equal and consecutive arcs $\sigma_1,
\tau_1, \dots, \tau_N, \sigma_N$, and for each of the arcs $\sigma_j$ we
construct a $C^\infty$-function $\omega_j$ on $[0, 2\pi]$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \label{itm:omega_j_positive_on_interior} $\omega_j(x) > 0$ on the
    interior of $\sigma_j$ while $\omega_j(x) = 0$ otherwise; and
    \item \label{itm:omega_j_derivative_bound} $|\omega_j'(x)| < \frac{1}{2}$
    for all $x$ and $j$
\end{enumerate}
(see Exercise~8.6.1). Now define the map
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Psi_T_definition}
    \Psi_T(z) = z \exp\left[i \sum_{j=1}^N t_j \omega_j(\theta)\right]
\end{equation}
where $z = r e^{i\theta}$ of $\Delta$ onto itself, and let the Beltrami
coefficient $\mu_T$ on $\Delta$ be the complex dilatation of $\Psi_T$.

The complex dilatation $\mu_T$ can easily be computed in terms of the partial
derivatives $\partial\Psi_T/\partial r$ and $\partial\Psi_T/\partial\theta$
(Exercise~8.6.2) and when this has been done we find that
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mu_T_computation}
    \mu_T(r e^{i\theta}) = \frac{e^{2i\theta} \sum_{j=1}^N t_j \omega_j(\theta)}{2 + \sum_{j=1}^N t_j \omega_j(\theta)},
\end{equation}
and hence for all $T$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mu_T_bound}
    |\mu_T(r e^{i\theta})| \leq \frac{\sum_{j=1}^N |t_j \omega_j(\theta)|}{2 - \sum_{j=1}^N |t_j \omega_j(\theta)|} \leq \frac{N\epsilon_1}{2 - N\epsilon_1}.
\end{equation}

Note that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item \label{itm:Psi_T_identity_on_tau} if $\arg z$ lies in any of the
    intervals $\tau_j$, then $\Psi_T(z) = z$;
    \item \label{itm:distinct_T_imply_distinct_Psi_T} distinct values of $T$
    lead to distinct functions $\Psi_T$; and
    \item \label{itm:mu_T_norm_bound_eta} given any $\eta$, $||\mu_T||_\infty <
    \eta$ if $\epsilon_1$ is small enough.
\end{enumerate}

The first two of these are the properties required of the function $\Psi_t$ at
the end of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}, and
by choosing $\epsilon_1$ small enough, \ref{itm:mu_T_norm_bound_eta} guarantees
that we can satisfy Lemma~\ref{lem:TO_DO_1}\ref{itm:lemma_to_do_1_a} for all
$\mu_T$. We fix this value of $\epsilon_1$, and it remains to complete the proof
of Lemma~\ref{lem:TO_DO_1}.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:TO_DO_1}]
    Given the Beltrami coefficient $\mu_T$, let the composition
    \eqref{eq:composite_map_mu_nu_varphi_varphiRvarphiminus1} be
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:composition_map_T}
        T \mapsto \mu_T \mapsto \nu_T \mapsto \varphi_T \mapsto R_T = \varphi_T R \varphi_T^{-1}.
    \end{equation}
    The idea of the proof is to factor the map $T \mapsto R_T$ through the
    intermediate stage of a vector whose components are the zeros and poles of
    $R_T$, and then to show that the first factor, and hence the composition, is
    constant on some curve.

    We may replace $R$ by any conjugate with respect to a Möbius map, so we may
    assume that $R$ is such that:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
        \item \label{itm:R_distinct_zeros} $R$ has distinct zeros $a_1, \dots,
        a_d$ in $\C$;
        \item \label{itm:R_distinct_poles} $R$ has distinct poles $b_1, \dots,
        b_d$ in $\C$; and
        \item \label{itm:R_origin_is_1} $R(0) = 1$.
    \end{enumerate}

    Now recall that $\varphi_T$ in \eqref{eq:composition_map_T} fixes $0$, $1$
    and $\infty$. It follows that $R_T$ is the unique rational function with
    zeros $\varphi_T(a_j)$, with poles $\varphi_T(b_j)$, and with $R_T(0) = 1$,
    and so we only have to show that the map
    \begin{equation*}
        \Pi(T) = (\varphi_T(a_1), \dots, \varphi_T(a_d), \varphi_T(b_1), \dots, \varphi_T(b_d))
    \end{equation*}
    of $(0, \epsilon_1)^N$ into $\C^{2d}$ is constant on some curve.

    Now in the fundamental paper \cite{Ahlfors1960}, Ahlfors and Bers examined
    the general question of how a solution $z \mapsto \varphi_t(z)$ of the
    Beltrami equation with coefficient $\mu_t(z)$ varies when the parameter $t$
    varies, and they showed that (in an appropriate sense) for each $z$, the map
    $t \mapsto \varphi_t(z)$ varies as smoothly as the map $t \mapsto \mu_t(z)$.
    We shall not enter into the details of this here, and we refer the
    interested reader to the original paper for the precise formulation of this
    result. For us, the important outcome is that because the map $T \mapsto
    \mu_T(z)$ is $C^\infty$, the same is true of $T \mapsto \nu_T(z)$ (in the
    sense of \cite{Ahlfors1960}), and hence each of the maps
    \begin{align*}
        t &\mapsto \varphi_T(a_j) \\
        t &\mapsto \varphi_T(b_j)
    \end{align*}
    is $C^\infty$. We conclude that $\Pi$ is a $C^\infty$-map of the open cube
    $\mathscr{C}_0 = (0, \epsilon_1)^N$ into $\C^{2d}$.

    The set of points where the rank of $\Pi'$ is maximal, say $k$, is an open
    subset of $\mathscr{C}_0$ (for the rank of $\Pi'$ is upper semi-continuous)
    and so the restriction of $\Pi$ to $\mathscr{C}_0$ is a map of constant
    rank. It follows from the Implicit Function Theorem (see, for example,
    \cite[79]{Boothby2003}) that the inverse image of a point is a sub-manifold
    of $\mathscr{C}_0$ of dimension $N - k$ (which is positive) and so there is
    some curve in $\mathscr{C}_0$ on which $\Pi$ is constant. The proof of the
    lemma, and of Theorem~\ref{thm:the_no_wandering_domains_theorem}, is now
    finished.
\end{proof}
